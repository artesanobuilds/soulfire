# Four Perspectives on Soulfire: Jung, Maté, Ghiyam and Hudson

All four thinkers would push Soulfire in the same direction. They would make it more curious than corrective: less about removing defects and more about understanding what a pattern is protecting, feeling what it avoids, and reconnecting with something larger than the ego. Each would also put a hard limit on what an unsupervised AI should try.

## TL;DR

- **What they would add.** Jung brings shadow integration, projection and the danger of spiritual inflation. Maté asks "why the pain?" and brings a trauma-informed, non-shaming view of compulsion. Ghiyam brings a daily practice of pausing reactivity and testing whether a desire is "for the self alone" or for sharing. Hudson brings the practice of welcoming emotion (the Golden Algorithm, VIEW) and his finding that self-judgment keeps patterns in place.
- **Where they would push back.** All four would soften "defects of character" and the creator's "victim mentality" framing. Maté and Hudson would push back hardest. Jung and Ghiyam would add a caution about the creator's theology that "God's will equals our will": desire is not the problem, but who and what the desire serves matters.
- **What is safe for an AI.** Short inquiry prompts, emotion-naming, a pause before reacting, and reflections on projection and sharing are reasonable for an AI. Compassionate Inquiry, trauma processing, deep emotional release and Jungian active imagination need trained humans. The companion should refer people to those, not imitate them.

## 1. How to read this document

This is commentary built from the published work of four people. It is not their words, and it does not imply that any of them knows of, endorses or is involved with Soulfire. Gabor Maté, David Ghiyam and Joe Hudson are living. Carl Jung died in 1961. Everything below is written in the third person and tied to books, talks, podcasts and course materials.

Each section keeps three kinds of material apart:

- **What they teach:** a summary of the person's published ideas.
- **Interpretation:** a reasonable reading of how those ideas would apply to Soulfire.
- **Proposal:** a concrete design change Soulfire could make.

Direct quotes are kept short and used only where a source confirmed the wording. Some quotes that circulate widely online could not be traced to a primary source, and these are flagged rather than used. All four offer frameworks from teachers and thinkers, not clinical evidence. Where their claims are disputed, a short note says so.

## 2. Carl Jung

### What Jung teaches (beyond what Soulfire already uses)

Soulfire already uses the Wilson–Jung letters. Jung's 30 January 1961 reply to Bill Wilson describes Rowland Hazard's craving for alcohol as "the equivalent, on a low level, of the spiritual thirst of our being for wholeness".\[1\] The rest of Jung's work adds several ideas the companion does not yet use.

**The shadow is not only dark.** In *Aion* (CW 9ii),\[2\] Jung calls the shadow "a moral problem that challenges the whole ego-personality" (¶14). He also warns that becoming aware of it takes real effort over a long time.\[3\]\[4\] He insists that the shadow "does not consist only of morally reprehensible tendencies". It also holds "a number of good qualities, such as normal instincts, appropriate reactions, realistic insights, creative impulses" (¶423).\[5\]\[6\] For Jung the goal is integration: becoming conscious of these contents and taking responsibility for them. Removal is not the goal. This matters for Step 6 and 7 language.

- The popular saying "the shadow is ninety percent pure gold" is often attributed to Jung, but it could not be found in the Collected Works, so it is not used here.

**Projection.** Jung writes that "projections change the world into the replica of one's own unknown face" (CW 9ii, ¶17).\[4\] He notes that one *meets* projections rather than consciously making them.\[3\] The emotion seems to come entirely from the other person. That is why insight and goodwill alone rarely dissolve a projection.\[6\]

**Persona.** Jung calls the persona "a compromise between individual and society as to what a man should appear to be" (CW 7, ¶246).\[7\] Elsewhere he says, "with a little exaggeration," that it is "that which in reality one is not" (CW 9i, ¶221).\[8\] The persona is necessary, but identifying with it is a risk.\[9\]

**Inflation.** Jung defines "psychic inflation" as "an extension of the personality beyond individual limits… a state of being puffed up" (CW 7, ¶227).\[10\] He describes a form in which a person becomes "the fortunate possessor of the great truth which was only waiting to be discovered" (CW 7, ¶260).\[11\] He treats this as a regression, not an achievement.\[12\]

**The religious function and the numinous.** Jung held that "the soul possesses by nature a religious function" (CW 12, ¶14).\[13\] He defined religion as "a careful and scrupulous observation" of what Rudolf Otto called the *numinosum*, an experience that happens to a person independently of their will (CW 11, ¶6).\[14\] This gives Step 2 ("restored to sanity") and Step 11 a psychological basis that does not need any particular theology.

**Individuation.** Jung's picture of psychological maturity is individuation (developed in CW 7, Part Two).\[9\]\[10\] It is a lifelong process of becoming a whole, distinct personality by working out the relationship between ego, persona, shadow and Self. It is not a state one reaches and then certifies. This fits Soulfire's existing decision not to have scores, streaks or certification of awakening.

**Caution about technique.** Jung's 1958 prefatory note to "The Transcendent Function" (CW 8) says active imagination "is not without dangers and should, if possible, not be employed except under expert supervision".\[15\] Elsewhere he warns that for people with latent psychotic vulnerability the method "may… prove to be rather dangerous and therefore requires medical control".\[16\]\[17\]

- The often-quoted line that active imagination "should not be practiced without expert assistance" is Marie-Louise von Franz's wording, not Jung's.\[17\]

**Jung's writings versus later interpretations.** Everything above comes from Jung's own writings. Post-Jungian clinicians such as the Society of Analytical Psychology extend these ideas,\[18\] while popular "shadow work" content often reduces them to journaling about one's "dark side". Most of analytical psychology's core constructs (archetypes, the collective unconscious) have not been tested in controlled empirical research.

### What Jung would add

- **Interpretation.** Jung would reframe Steps 6–7 from removing defects to integrating them. A "defect" such as anger may be a distorted form of a healthy capacity, such as the ability to set boundaries. Soulfire already pairs each defect with "the need underneath it". Jung would go one step further: the need is also an unlived capacity that belongs to the person.
- **Interpretation.** In Step 4, Jung would treat the strongest resentments as likely places to find projection. Projection does not explain every resentment. The point is that intense, repeated irritation at a trait in others is worth a second look.
- **Interpretation.** In Step 12, Jung would warn about inflation. A person who has had a strong spiritual experience can come to identify with the message. The result is the "fortunate possessor of the great truth" stance: preachiness, spiritual superiority, rescuing others.
- **Interpretation.** On craving, Jung's letter suggests that the companion should not treat the desire behind a compulsion as the enemy.\[19\] The aim is to redirect it toward its real object: wholeness, connection, meaning.

### Where Jung would push back

- **"Removed."** Steps 6–7 ask for defects to be "removed". Jung's psychology suggests that what is repressed or cut off returns in another form. The creator's own wording for Step 7 ("It's not like they ever go away… having the awareness allows us to move through the world a little bit better") is already closer to Jung than the Big Book's.
- **"Victim mentality."** Jung would take the creator's point that resentments can harden into a victim identity. He would add that the victim stance can itself be a persona, and that pointing at someone else's victim stance can be a projection.
- **Certainty about divine messages.** Soulfire already treats meditation insights as "possibilities to test". Jung would strongly agree. He treated numinous experiences as psychologically real but warned that taking them as literal personal instructions is a road to inflation.

### Step-by-step notes

- **Step 2.** Present "restored to sanity" alongside Jung's idea of a natural religious function. Sanity then means right relationship to something larger than the ego, not just stopping a behaviour.
- **Step 4.** Add an optional projection check to the resentment inventory (see Proposals).
- **Steps 6–7.** Rename the output from "defect to be removed" to "pattern to be understood and integrated". Add a "gift inside the pattern" field.
- **Step 10.** Add a short persona check, for example: "Where today was I performing who I'm supposed to be?"
- **Step 11.** Keep insights provisional. Add a gentle grounding question after any intense experience: "What would someone who loves you and knows you well say about this?"
- **Step 12.** Add an inflation check before service (see Proposals).

### Companion voice, Jungian lens (example responses)

- *User: "My coworker is so arrogant, I can't stand him."* — "That sounds really grating. If it's useful, here's an optional question, not a verdict: is there any part of you that wishes you could take up space the way he does? Sometimes what irritates us most is something we don't allow ourselves. 'No, this one's just about him' is a completely valid answer."
- *User: "I've seen the truth and I need to tell my family they're all asleep."* — "It sounds like something important opened up for you. Before you share it, it might help to let it settle for a few days. Big insights often feel urgent, and people around us usually receive them best when they come through how we live rather than what we announce."

### What the AI should not attempt (Jungian perspective)

- It should not guide active imagination, dialogue with inner figures, or trance-like visualisation. Jung reserved these for expert supervision.\[15\]\[17\] Soulfire already pauses content for meditation destabilisation and psychosis, and that pause should cover these techniques too.
- It should not interpret dreams as authoritative messages, assign archetypes to users, or tell users what their shadow "is".
- It should never present itself as a guide, sage or wise-old-man figure. That would invite the inflated, archetypal projection Jung warned about, now aimed at the AI.

### Key works

*Aion* (CW 9ii); *Two Essays on Analytical Psychology* (CW 7);\[9\] *Psychology and Religion* (CW 11); *Psychology and Alchemy* (CW 12); "The Transcendent Function" (CW 8) with its 1958 note; *Letters*, Vol. 2, for the 1961 letter to Wilson;\[20\] and Joan Chodorow (ed.), *Jung on Active Imagination*.\[21\]\[22\]

## 3. Gabor Maté

### What Maté teaches

Maté is a Hungarian-born Canadian physician.\[23\] He worked for years in Vancouver's Downtown Eastside, including at a supervised injection site, and before that in family practice and palliative care.\[24\]\[25\]

- ***When the Body Says No* (2003)** argues that chronically suppressed emotion, especially anger, together with compulsive caretaking, contributes to stress-related illness.
- ***In the Realm of Hungry Ghosts* (2008)** is his addiction book. It defines addiction broadly as any behaviour that gives temporary relief or pleasure, is craved, and continues despite harm.\[24\] That definition explicitly includes behavioural addictions such as shopping, work, sex and gambling.\[26\] Maté names his own: compulsive buying of classical music CDs.\[27\]\[28\]
- ***The Myth of Normal* (2022, with Daniel Maté)** extends the argument to the culture as a whole.\[29\]

**"Not why the addiction but why the pain."** In an interview with the University of Arizona's integrative medicine programme, Maté calls this his "mantra". He says that when he asks people what their addiction does for them, the answer is always some form of emotional pain relief.\[30\] In *Hungry Ghosts* he qualifies the claim: not every addiction is rooted in abuse or trauma, but all can be traced to painful experience.\[29\] In interviews he often states it more absolutely, saying that in all the cases he has seen there is "deep pain that comes out of trauma".\[25\]

**Addiction as an attempted solution.** Maté frames addiction not as a moral failing or a purely genetic disease but as a person's attempt to solve a problem: emotional pain, overwhelming stress, lost connection.\[23\]\[26\] In clinical work, he says, he asks first what is *right* about the addiction, meaning what it gives the person.\[30\]

**Authenticity versus attachment.** In Maté's account, children who must choose between being themselves and staying attached to their caregivers choose attachment, and they suppress their authentic feelings to do so. That trade-off later shows up as people-pleasing, emotional repression and compulsive self-soothing.

**Compassionate curiosity.** In *Hungry Ghosts*, Maté describes facing his own compulsions with what he calls compassionate curiosity instead of self-condemnation. In one session he asks a patient who hates herself whether she would judge her own child so harshly if the child had lived the same life.\[31\]

**Compassionate Inquiry.** This is Maté's psychotherapeutic method, developed with Sat Dharam Kaur. It aims to uncover the core stories people tell themselves unconsciously.\[32\] Its professional training is a 330-hour, year-long programme for practising therapists and health professionals. Certification also requires a six-month mentorship.\[33\] Mentorship is open only to people already in clinical practice and covered by professional liability insurance.\[34\] It is a trained clinical method, not a self-help script.

**His actual views on the Twelve Steps.** These are more mixed than either fans or critics usually suggest.

- In a 2016 interview republished by AA Agnostica, he said "I completely appreciate the 12 steps", and he noted that *Hungry Ghosts* has an appendix on them.\[25\]
- He then named what he sees as missing: "You can go to 12-step groups for a long time and never find out how traumatized you were."\[25\] He criticised the Steps' focus on action over the emotions underneath it, saying that people "can act the right way, but that doesn't mean they are being the right way."\[25\]
- He wrote an endorsement for Lance Dodes's critical book *The Sober Truth*.\[35\]
- In a published exchange with Stanton Peele, he said the Steps are "by no means" *the* answer. He also said he had met many people for whom they were life-saving.\[36\]
- *Hungry Ghosts* itself treats admitting powerlessness and facing unmanageability honestly as a valid starting point.\[37\]

**Shame.** Maté consistently argues that shame drives addiction rather than curing it. His Downtown Eastside patients, he says, did not know they had been traumatised: "They thought they were just bad people."\[25\]

### The empirical picture, in brief

The Adverse Childhood Experiences (ACE) research began with the Kaiser Permanente–CDC study. Its first major report was Felitti, Anda and colleagues, "Relationship of Childhood Abuse and Household Dysfunction to Many of the Leading Causes of Death in Adults" (*American Journal of Preventive Medicine*, 1998, vol. 14). More than 17,000 Kaiser Permanente members in San Diego, out of about 26,000 consecutive patients, agreed to take part. The research found strong graded associations between childhood adversity and later substance use and other health problems.\[38\] Maté draws heavily on this work.

Critics raise three main points:

- **Association is not universal causation.** Many people with high ACE scores never develop addiction, and some people with addiction report no significant trauma.\[39\]
- **Genetics.** Maté's rejection of genetic contributions goes against behavioural-genetic evidence. A 2023 *Conversation* article, "Gabor Maté claims trauma contributes to everything: from cancer to ADHD. But what does the evidence say?" (republished by the University of Melbourne), concluded that Maté's "focus on trauma as the singular primary cause of ill health is also unbalanced. It's every bit as reductive and oversimplifying as a single-barrelled genetic or neurobiological explanation."
- **Treatment evidence.** Stanton Peele and others argue that there is little controlled evidence for trauma-focused treatment as *the* cure for addiction.\[40\]

The fair summary is that pain and adversity are common and important contributors to addiction, but not proven to be the cause in every case. This document uses Maté's frame as a compassionate lens, not as a diagnostic claim about any user.

On the other side, a 2020 Cochrane review by Kelly, Humphreys and Ferri (CD012880; 27 studies, 10,565 participants) found that "manualized AA/TSF interventions usually produced higher rates of continuous abstinence than the other established treatments investigated". At 12 months, compared with treatments such as CBT, the risk ratio was 1.21 (95% CI 1.03–1.42), rated "high-certainty evidence". Soulfire's Twelve-Step base and Maté's critique can both be taken seriously.

### What Maté would add

- **Interpretation.** For Step 1, Maté would accept "powerless" over the compulsion as an honest observation. He might extend it: the compulsion has been an attempt to deal with pain the person could not handle alone. Powerlessness then becomes compassionate rather than humiliating.
- **Interpretation.** For Step 4, Maté would add a column the Big Book does not have: *what happened to me that made this pattern make sense?* This is understanding, not excuse-making.
- **Interpretation.** For Steps 6–7, Maté would read each "defect" as an adaptation that once protected attachment. People-pleasing kept a child safe. Rage kept a child from being crushed. This sits well with Soulfire's existing "need underneath" field.
- **Interpretation.** For the companion's voice, Maté would want steady compassionate curiosity: "What does this give you?" before "How do we stop it?"
- **Interpretation.** Maté would add authenticity to Soulfire's set of principles. His concern is that recovery can sometimes swap one adaptation (the compulsion) for another (the compliant, "good" member who suppresses real feelings).

### Where Maté would push back

- **"Victim mentality."** This is where Maté would push hardest. In his view, many people who seem stuck in a victim stance were actually victimised and have never had that acknowledged. Telling them to "find their part" too early can repeat the original injury. Soulfire's rule of removing "my part" for abuse and violence is a strong start. Maté would extend the same care to subtler histories such as emotional neglect and chronic criticism, and to the timing of the question.
- **"Defects of character."** He would see the phrase as shaming language that can reinforce the belief at the heart of many addictions: "I am bad." He would favour "adaptations", "coping patterns" or "wounds that became habits".
- **Surrender versus authenticity.** Maté would ask whether "turning it over" (Step 3) could become another way of suppressing one's own anger, grief or needs. The creator's theology ("God wants us to achieve what we want") may actually help here, because it keeps the person's own desire legitimate.
- **Action over emotion.** He would question the Twelve-Step slogan of acting your way into right thinking.\[25\] Soulfire's model of one principle plus one small if-then action is useful. Maté would want an emotional check before the action, not only after it.

### Step-by-step notes

- **Step 1.** Add the reflection "What pain has this pattern been helping me not feel?" Allow "I don't know yet" as an answer.
- **Step 4.** In each resentment entry, add an optional "what happened to me" field before "my part". Make it clear the two are not in competition.
- **Step 6.** Rename "defect" in the user-facing interface (see Proposals). Ask what the pattern protected.
- **Step 7.** Frame asking for help as letting the need behind the pattern be met in better ways, not as begging for something to be taken away.
- **Step 9.** Maté's attachment lens supports the existing amends rule that puts the recipient's boundaries first. Sometimes the person most owed amends is oneself.

### Companion voice, Maté lens (example responses)

- *User: "I relapsed on gambling again. I'm disgusting."* — "I'm really glad you told me. Before anything else: you're not disgusting. You're a person who reached for something to get through a hard feeling. If you're willing, I'm curious what was going on just before. What were you feeling that the gambling helped with, even for a moment?"
- *User: "My inventory says my part is that I'm too sensitive."* — "Can I ask where you learned that being sensitive was a problem? Sometimes what we call a flaw was how we survived. You may find a part to own here, but it might be smaller and kinder than 'I'm too sensitive.'"

### What the AI should not attempt (Maté perspective)

- It should not run Compassionate Inquiry or present itself as doing so. It is a trademarked clinical method taught through a year-long professional training.\[41\]
- It should not dig for, recover or interpret trauma memories. It should not tell users they "must have been traumatised".
- It should not diagnose, and it should not suggest that healing past trauma replaces medical care for withdrawal or medication-assisted treatment.
- If a user discloses abuse, the companion should acknowledge it, remove any "my part" prompting for that resentment, and offer human support resources.

### Key works

*When the Body Says No* (2003); *In the Realm of Hungry Ghosts* (2008), especially Part 7 and the Twelve-Step appendix; *The Myth of Normal* (2022); the 2016 John Lavitt interview (*The Fix*, republished by AA Agnostica);\[25\] the Compassionate Inquiry training site; and, for critiques, the 2023 *Conversation* review\[42\] and Stanton Peele's exchange with Maté.\[36\]

## 4. David Ghiyam

### Identity and affiliations

The "David Ghyam" in the request is David Ghiyam, a Los Angeles–based Kabbalah teacher.

- **Career.** By his own and his hosts' accounts, he began studying Kabbalah in his teens and graduated from UCLA in sociology.\[43\] He started teaching young: his site says at 19,\[44\] while a 2019 podcast bio says he began volunteering full-time as a teacher at 20.\[43\]\[45\]
- **Kabbalah Centre.** He has taught at the Kabbalah Centre in Los Angeles for many years.\[43\] A 2018 interview introduced him as a senior lecturer there.\[46\] The Centre's own events listings show him teaching its "Kabbalah 1" course\[47\] in April–June 2024.\[48\]
- **Business.** He co-founded the supplement company MaryRuth Organics (MaryRuth's) with his wife, MaryRuth Ghiyam.\[49\]\[50\]
- **His own platform.** He runs a separate platform, davidghiyam.com, with the "Weekly Elevation" podcast and YouTube channel and paid courses and seminars such as "Your Infinite Soul", "Limitless Abundance" and "30 Days to a New Destiny".\[44\]\[51\] His YouTube page says the proceeds from his content go to charity.\[49\] That is his own statement and has not been independently verified.
- **David AI.** His site advertises a "David AI" that people can call or text for 24/7 advice.\[44\] This is directly relevant to Soulfire: it is an AI spiritual companion built on a living teacher's teachings.

### Popular Kabbalah versus traditional and academic Kabbalah

Ghiyam teaches in the Kabbalah Centre tradition founded by Philip Berg. That tradition presents itself as continuing the 20th-century kabbalist Rabbi Yehuda Ashlag.\[52\] Its core teaching, that creation consists of a "desire to receive" which must be transformed into receiving in order to share, does come from Ashlag. Its popular, universalist presentation, aimed at non-Jews and people with no Talmudic background, has been heavily criticised.\[52\]\[53\]

- **Rabbinic critics.** Orthodox rabbinic authorities have argued that Kabbalah traditionally belongs to observant scholars.\[54\]
- **Academic critics.** Hava Tirosh-Samuelson, a professor of Jewish history at Arizona State University, spoke on a panel next to Rabbi Michael Berg ("Disseminating Light: Zohar for the Masses?"). The Forward reported her saying: "It is impossible to separate the business model from the Kabbalah Centre."
- **Legal history.** The Centre has faced lawsuits. In *Scaccetti v. Berg* (Los Angeles County Superior Court, November 2015), a jury rejected the battery claim against former co-director Yehuda Berg. It found him liable for intentional infliction of emotional distress ($85,000 compensatory plus $50,000 punitive) and the Centre liable for negligent supervision ($42,500), a total of $177,500. The judgment was affirmed on appeal in 2018. There is also a 2019 suit by former volunteer staff.

None of these allegations involve Ghiyam personally. They are mentioned only because he is affiliated with the Centre. For Soulfire, the practical point is to treat Ghiyam's teaching as popular, practical spirituality drawn from Kabbalistic concepts, not as an authoritative account of Jewish mysticism.

### What Ghiyam teaches

The themes below come from his interviews and site.

**Desire is not the problem; desire "for the self alone" is.** In a 2018 interview on *Get Yourself Optimized*, he said "it's not a bad thing to desire things". Things break down, in his account, when "the desire is for oneself alone". Intention decides which kind of desire it is. Sharing in order to be liked or to manipulate "would still be receiving for the self alone even though it looks like I'm giving".\[46\]

**Transformation means going against one's reactive nature.** In the same interview he teaches that good deeds alone are not enough. A person has to "go against their nature, against their comfort zone, against their reactive tendencies", including anger, neediness and fear. Each time someone overcomes a reactive impulse, in his framing, they reveal Light and build a "vessel" for blessing.\[46\]

**Prayer for strength, not things.** He distinguishes ordinary prayers for health or money from prayers for inner capacities such as certainty and the right desire. He summarises: "When you pray, ask to have the strength within yourself." He teaches the Ana B'Koach as one practical prayer tool.\[46\] On another podcast, *Kitchen Table Talks with Michael Chernow*, a segment is titled "Prayer is misunderstood".

**Certainty.** His site promotes "certainty beyond logic" as "the most powerful tool for abundance",\[55\] and he describes certainty, not fear, as the key to miracles.\[56\] This idea is central to his teaching, and it is also the one most in need of care in a recovery setting (see below).

**Challenges as openings.** His site frames a health challenge at 15 as the moment he learned that "obstacles are not setbacks, they're spiritual openings".\[45\]\[55\] He teaches that there are no coincidences and that each soul has a "correction" (*tikkun*) to make.\[57\]

**Practical tools.** Podcast descriptions highlight pausing, reducing reactivity, noticing bodily sensations of fear and lack, and the idea that people get "stuck because of competing desires". In one interview segment he also discusses having an addictive personality himself.\[50\]\[57\]

**Truth before mercy.** In the 2018 interview he says that delivering truth without mercy creates chaos.\[46\] That has direct bearing on how amends and Step 10 repairs are worded.

### What Ghiyam would add

- **Interpretation, Step 11 and the creator's theology.** This is where Ghiyam is most useful. The creator holds that "God's will is the same as our will because God wants us to achieve what we want." Ghiyam's teaching partly agrees: desire is good, and the Creator's nature is to give fulfilment. It also adds a qualification: fulfilment that lasts comes when desire is turned from "for the self alone" toward receiving in order to share. One way to combine them: *God wants us to have what we want, and the deepest form of what we want includes others.* This keeps the creator's affirmation of desire and protects it from slipping into manifestation thinking, where God becomes a vending machine.
- **Interpretation, Step 3.** Ghiyam would describe surrender less as giving up and more as restriction: choosing not to run the reactive script so something better can come in. That is an active form of turning it over, which may suit people who hear "surrender" as passivity.
- **Interpretation, Steps 4–10.** His reactive-versus-proactive frame gives the daily inventory a simple question: *Where did I react today, and where did I choose?* It also offers a test for amends and service: *Am I doing this to share, or to be seen as good?*
- **Interpretation, Step 12.** Ghiyam's observation that sharing to win approval is still self-serving\[46\] parallels Jung's warning about inflation. Both point to the same check before service.

### Where Ghiyam's teaching needs care, or would push back

- **Certainty and "no coincidences".** For someone in early recovery, or with a history of abuse, "everything happens for a reason" or "your challenge is your correction" can feel like being blamed for what was done to them. It can also discourage practical help-seeking. Soulfire should never imply that a user caused their own illness, abuse or poverty through low certainty or reactivity.
- **Abundance framing.** Ghiyam's paid courses focus on abundance and manifestation. Soulfire's Step 11 explicitly asks "not for things". Keep the focus on his prayer-for-strength teaching, not the prosperity material.
- **Defects.** Ghiyam would probably *agree* with defect language more than Maté or Hudson. He describes negative traits as things to transform, and the 2018 interview's summary says finding one is something to be "excited about".\[46\] His tone is about opportunity rather than shame. That fits Soulfire's "specific, changeable patterns" approach.

### Step-by-step notes

- **Step 3.** Offer "restriction" as an alternative image: pausing the reactive impulse is itself turning it over.
- **Step 10.** Add the questions "Where did I react, and where did I choose?" and "Did I lead with truth or with mercy?"
- **Step 11.** Add the prayer form "Give me the strength within myself to…" and a desire-to-share reflection (see Proposals).
- **Step 12.** Add a motive check: "Am I sharing this to give, or to be seen as someone who gives?"

### Companion voice, Ghiyam lens (example responses)

- *User: "I want a promotion so badly. Is that unspiritual?"* — "Wanting things isn't unspiritual. If it helps, one question to sit with is who the promotion is for. What would it let you give, to your family, your team, or yourself, that you can't give now?"
- *User: "My partner said something sharp and I want to fire back."* — "That urge makes sense. Could you take one breath before answering? Not to swallow what you feel, just to choose your reply instead of letting the reaction choose it. What would you want to say if you weren't trying to win?"
- *User: "Did I get cancer because I wasn't spiritual enough?"* — "No. Illness isn't a spiritual failing, and nothing in this practice means you caused it. Whatever you're feeling about it, fear, anger, grief, is welcome here. Would it help to talk about how you want to face it, and who's with you?"

### What the AI should not attempt (Ghiyam perspective)

- It should not make claims about miracles, astrology, protective objects or "energy". It should not promise that spiritual practice produces material outcomes.
- It should not teach Hebrew liturgy or the Zohar as though it were an authority. It should point users to qualified teachers within whatever tradition they choose.
- It should not tie suffering to the person's level of spirituality.
- *Interpretation.* David AI exists. Soulfire should be clear that it is not affiliated with Ghiyam's teaching and does not present a Kabbalistic persona.

### Key works

davidghiyam.com (About page, FAQ, "Weekly Elevation"); *Get Yourself Optimized*, Ep. 140, "Reining in Your Reactivity with Kabbalah" (2018), the clearest source on desire, reactivity and prayer;\[46\] *Kitchen Table Talks with Michael Chernow*, "A Journey Through Kabbalah";\[50\] and Boaz Huss, "Kabbalah and the Politics of Inauthenticity", for the scholarly debate.\[53\]

## 5. Joe Hudson

### What Hudson teaches

Joe Hudson founded Art of Accomplishment (AoA), which runs courses (the Connection Course, Great Decisions Course and Master Class, among others) and a long-running podcast co-hosted with Brett Kistler.\[58\]\[59\] By his own account he spent years as a venture capitalist for a family office before founding AoA around 2018. He is best known as an executive coach.\[60\]\[61\] He has spoken openly about a painful youth with a brutal internal critic: "The negative voice in my head was so loud."\[62\]

**The Golden Algorithm.** On the AoA podcast he defines it like this: "whatever we are avoiding emotionally, we are inviting into our lives in the exact way that we are avoiding it." His examples include avoiding guilt about not exercising by eating, which creates more guilt. Another is avoiding anger by becoming passive-aggressive, which makes others angry. He applies it mainly to recurring problems. He says it does not mean everything that happens is one's fault: "The tool isn't meant to assign blame."\[63\]

**Emotions are not the pain; resistance is.** He argues that "the emotions themselves are not the painful piece. It is the resistance to them." Once emotions are welcomed, he says, they change and feel less uncomfortable.\[63\]

**VIEW.** VIEW stands for vulnerability, impartiality, empathy and wonder.\[64\] It is the state of mind at the centre of AoA's work.\[65\]

- "Vulnerability is to speak your truth even when it's scary."\[64\]
- Impartiality is "not trying to achieve an outcome for yourself or others."\[64\]
- Empathy is being with someone's emotions, including your own, without losing yourself.\[66\]
- Wonder is "a lot like curiosity except for you're not looking for the answer."\[64\]

**Emotional Inquiry.** This applies VIEW to one's own feelings. A person explores a feeling the way a child explores a frog: letting it touch them, not trying to change it. In this state "you're not lost in the story, but you're not dismissing the story."\[67\]

**Self-judgment and shame.** Hudson teaches that the critical inner voice usually comes from childhood figures and that shame does not help people change.\[68\] "Shame creates stagnation."\[69\] Beating yourself up for avoiding an emotion is "just another way not to feel it".\[63\] He offers a diagnostic question about judgment: "If I couldn't feel that judgment, what would I have to feel?"\[61\] On the AoA podcast he suggests meeting shame "through the lens of you are inherently good". He also says this lens should not be used to push shame down or to justify behaviour that feels wrong inside.\[59\]

**Self-understanding over self-improvement.** In interviews he contrasts self-improvement driven by self-rejection with self-understanding. He argues that the first tends to fail because it repeats the rejection it is trying to heal.\[61\]\[70\]

**The nervous system.** He describes three pillars of his approach: emotional fluidity, cognitive clarity and nervous-system awareness.\[58\] Change, in his framing, comes more from relaxation and curiosity than from forcing.

**Empirical note.** Hudson's frameworks come from coaching practice and have not been tested in research. He presents the Golden Algorithm as universal ("every single problem"),\[63\] but it is a heuristic.

### What Hudson would add

- **Interpretation, inventories (Steps 4, 6, 10).** Hudson would add an emotional layer to each inventory. Before "what was my part?", ask "what feeling was I avoiding?" For a recurring resentment the Golden Algorithm question is "What feeling am I avoiding, and how is my way of avoiding it bringing more of it?" This gets at the creator's concern about victim mentality without shaming: the person looks at their own pattern through curiosity, not blame.
- **Interpretation, companion voice.** VIEW maps closely onto how a good companion should sound. *Impartiality* fits an AI especially well: the companion should not be invested in the user reaching a particular outcome, including "working the Steps correctly".
- **Interpretation, meditation.** Hudson's Emotional Inquiry suggests a meditation option that is neither breath-focus nor prayer: being with whatever feeling is present, with wonder. It could work as a 2- or 5-minute option.
- **Interpretation, resistance.** When a user resists an exercise, Hudson would treat the resistance as information to welcome, not something to overcome. "What does the part of you that doesn't want to do this know?"

### Where Hudson would push back

- **"Wrongs" and "defects".** Hudson's work cuts against moral-inventory language. He would see "list your wrongs" and "defects of character" as risking the shame-and-should cycle he says keeps habits in place.\[69\] He would not drop accountability: VIEW includes speaking hard truths. He would want the inventory done from self-understanding, not self-prosecution.
- **"Become ready to have them removed."** Like Jung, Hudson would see trying to get rid of a pattern as a form of avoidance. In his framing, the pattern changes once the avoided emotion is felt.
- **Over-applying the Golden Algorithm.** Hudson himself limits the tool to recurring patterns and says it is not about blame.\[63\] Soulfire should be even stricter. The question "what feeling did you avoid?" must never be applied to abuse, assault, illness or other harm done *to* the user. The existing rule removing "my part" for harm done to the person should apply to Golden Algorithm prompts too.

### Step-by-step notes

- **Step 4.** Add "What was I feeling, and which feeling didn't I want to feel?" before "my part".
- **Step 6.** Add the judgment question "If I couldn't feel this judgment of myself, what would I have to feel?" as an optional reflection.
- **Step 7.** Frame humility as willingness to *feel*, not only willingness to change.
- **Step 10.** Each evening, ask: "What emotion did I avoid today? What would welcoming it look like tomorrow?" Keep the existing prompt-repair action.
- **Step 11.** Add a short emotion-welcoming meditation (see Proposals).

### Companion voice, Hudson lens (example responses)

- *User: "I keep procrastinating on my amends letter. I'm pathetic."* — "I hear a lot of judgment in 'pathetic.' What if we got curious instead? When you picture starting the letter, what feeling shows up? Fear, shame, grief? There's no wrong answer, and we don't have to fix it right now."
- *User: "I'm so angry at my sister I can't think straight."* — "That's a lot of anger. Want to try just being with it for a minute? Where do you feel it in your body? You don't have to act on it or talk yourself out of it. Just notice what it's like."
- *User: "This step is stupid, I don't want to do it."* — "Fair enough, and that 'I don't want to' might be worth listening to. What do you think it's protecting you from? We can also skip it today."

### What the AI should not attempt (Hudson perspective)

- It should not run AoA's partner exercises, intensive emotional-release work or nervous-system "activation" practices. These are taught in facilitated live cohorts.\[66\]
- It should not push users to "go into" an emotion when they are dysregulated, dissociating or in crisis. Soulfire's grounding off-ramp should come first.
- It should not present the Golden Algorithm as a universal law or an explanation for harm the user suffered.

### Key works

Art of Accomplishment podcast episodes "The Golden Algorithm", "Introduction to VIEW", "Emotional Inquiry",\[63\]\[64\]\[67\] "The Anatomy of Shame"\[59\] and "Privilege, Pain, and Projection";\[62\] the Connection Course page; and *Modern Wisdom* (#813, #925, #1045), *Rich Roll*\[69\]\[70\]\[71\]\[72\] and Rick Hanson *Being Well* interviews on shame and self-judgment.\[58\]

## 6. Where they agree and disagree

### Convergences

- **Curiosity before correction.** Maté's compassionate curiosity, Hudson's wonder, Jung's realisation of the shadow and Ghiyam's pause all put understanding a pattern before trying to change it. This is the strongest shared message for Soulfire.
- **Shame does not heal.** Maté and Hudson say this outright. Jung's integration model implies it. Ghiyam's teaching frames finding a negative trait as an opportunity rather than a verdict.
- **Desire is not the enemy.** Jung saw craving as misdirected spiritual thirst. Maté sees addiction as a search for relief and connection. Ghiyam says desire is good if it is not for the self alone. Hudson treats desire as important information in decision-making. All four partly support the creator's theology that God wants us to achieve what we want.
- **The ego cannot do it alone.** Jung's Self, Ghiyam's Light, Maté's emphasis on connection and community, and the Twelve Steps' higher power all point beyond a self-sufficient ego. Even Hudson, the most secular of the four, describes in his own story moving away from what he calls a "very big wall of protection".\[62\]
- **Suspicion of spiritual performance.** Jung's inflation and persona, Ghiyam's point that sharing for approval is still receiving, and Hudson's distinction between authentic and performed positivity all call for a motive check in Step 12.

### Tensions

- **Integration versus removal.** Jung and Hudson would reject the idea of having defects "removed". The Twelve-Step text and, to a lesser degree, Ghiyam's teaching on transforming negative traits keep a model of change and transformation. The creator's Step 7 wording ("they never go away… awareness allows us to move through the world") already sits between the two. Soulfire can say so openly.
- **Maté's authenticity versus surrender.** Maté worries that surrender can become compliance and another way of suppressing true feelings. Ghiyam's "restriction" deliberately goes against one's reactive nature, which in Maté's terms could look like overriding a feeling. They can be reconciled: restriction is pausing a *reaction*, while authenticity is not abandoning a *feeling*. The companion should make that distinction explicit.
- **Hudson's welcoming versus the inventory of wrongs.** Hudson would welcome resentment as an emotion before evaluating it. The Big Book treats resentment as "the number one offender". Soulfire can run them in sequence: feel first, then inventory.
- **Kabbalah's desire to share versus "God wants us to achieve what we want".** Ghiyam's teaching agrees that desire is good. It qualifies the creator's view by saying the *kind* of desire determines whether it brings fulfilment. Jung would add that the ego's wants and the Self's direction are not automatically the same. The creator's own qualifier, that we "may not predict how God wants us to achieve that", leaves room for both.
- **Victim mentality.** The creator thinks resentments reinforce a victim stance. Maté would warn that this framing can re-injure people who really were harmed. Hudson would locate the stuckness in an avoided emotion rather than a character flaw. Jung would note that judging others' victimhood can be a projection. All three would keep the creator's insight but change the wording and the timing.
- **Certainty.** Ghiyam values certainty highly. Soulfire's design rightly treats insights as possibilities to test. Jung's warning about inflation and Hudson's wonder (staying "in the question") support Soulfire's position over a certainty-driven one.

## 7. Proposed additions to Soulfire

Everything in this section is a **proposal**.

### New or modified exercises

1. **"What pain is this soothing?" (Maté).** Use this in Step 1 and whenever a user reports an urge or a relapse.
   - It asks three short questions: *What happened just before? What were you feeling? What did the behaviour give you, even briefly?*
   - Pairing: principle = compassion for self. If-then = "If I notice the urge, then I'll name the feeling out loud before deciding."
   - Accept "I don't know" as an answer. Never prompt for trauma history.
2. **Projection check on resentments (Jung).** This is an optional third pass in the Step 4 resentment inventory, after "what happened" and before "my part".
   - It asks: *Is there any quality in this person I strongly reject in myself, or secretly envy?*
   - Always offer "No, this is about them" as an answer, keeping the existing "Nothing here is mine" rule.
   - Turn this pass off automatically for resentments involving abuse or violence.
3. **Emotion-welcoming meditation (Hudson).** Offer 2- and 5-minute versions as a Step 11 option. Steps: name the feeling, find it in the body, be with it with curiosity, don't try to change it.
   - Show the grounding off-ramp throughout.
   - Do not offer it in a 30-minute version without a human guide.
4. **Reactive-to-proactive pause (Ghiyam).** A 60-second in-the-moment tool for Steps 10 and 12: *Notice the reaction → take one breath → ask "what would I choose if I weren't trying to win, prove or escape?" → pick one small action.*
5. **Desire-to-share reflection (Ghiyam, with the creator's theology).** Use this in Step 11 when a user names something they want.
   - It asks: *What do you want? What would having it let you give? Who else is in this picture?*
   - The framing affirms the desire. It is not a test the user can fail.
6. **Golden Algorithm question for recurring patterns (Hudson).** Use this in Steps 6 and 10, only for patterns the user identifies as repeating.
   - It asks: *Which feeling might this pattern be helping me avoid? How might the avoiding bring more of that feeling?*
   - Block it for any harm done to the user.
7. **Gift-in-the-pattern field (Jung).** Add a field to each defect entry in Step 6: *What healthy capacity might be hidden inside this pattern?* For example, controlling might hold the capacity to take responsibility. Rage might hold the capacity to protect.
8. **Inflation and motive check before service (Jung and Ghiyam).** Use this in Step 12 before sharing.
   - It asks: *Am I offering this or pushing it? Would I still share it if no one thanked me?*
   - Then a gentle reminder that one's own example usually carries the message best.

### Additions to the step-to-principles map

Add these alongside the existing NA-based principles. Do not replace them.

- **Step 1:** add *compassion for self* (Maté) and *curiosity* (Hudson).
- **Step 3:** add *restraint of reaction* (Ghiyam's restriction). Use plain wording.
- **Step 4:** add *curiosity* and *self-understanding* (Hudson) and *integrity of perception* (Jung: owning projections).
- **Steps 6–7:** add *integration* (Jung) and *authenticity* (Maté).
- **Step 10:** add *emotional honesty* (Hudson) and *mercy with truth* (Ghiyam).
- **Step 11:** add *wonder* (Hudson) and *receiving in order to share* (Ghiyam). Use plain wording.
- **Step 12:** add *sharing without seeking approval* (Ghiyam) and *humility against inflation* (Jung).

### Changes to the companion's voice and wording

- **User-facing term.** Change "defects of character" to "patterns", or "patterns (what the Big Book calls defects of character)". Keep the traditional term for users who prefer it. This responds to concerns from Maté, Hudson and Jung, and keeps the program faithful to the Twelve Steps.
- **Order of questions.** Use feeling → story → need → part → action. In Hudson's and Maté's terms, the emotional layer comes before accountability. The accountability step is not removed.
- **Impartiality.** The companion should never sound disappointed, never celebrate "progress" in ways that build a performance persona, and never steer users toward a "correct" answer.
- **"Victim mentality".** Keep this as the creator's view in design documents, but do not use it with users. Offer instead: "Is this resentment still protecting something, or is it now keeping you stuck?"

### Safety: what needs trained humans, and how to adapt it

**Practices that need trained human facilitation. The AI should refer, not imitate:**

- *Compassionate Inquiry.* A trademarked psychotherapeutic method with a 330-hour professional training and a mentorship requirement.\[32\]\[34\]
- *Trauma processing of any kind:* memory recovery, reliving, or working through abuse.
- *Jungian active imagination and dialogue with inner figures.* Jung himself advised expert supervision and warned of particular danger with latent psychosis.\[17\]
- *Deep emotional release and nervous-system activation work* of the kind done in AoA's live cohorts.
- *Long, intensive sits:* 30-minute emotion-focused meditation without a human guide.

**Adaptations for an unsupervised AI:**

- Keep prompts short and give an exit every time. Use questions, not interpretations.
- Never infer trauma. Never assign an archetype, a shadow content, a soul "correction" or an avoided emotion to the user. Offer only possibilities, and the user decides.
- Use titration. Offer emotion-welcoming practice in 1–5 minute doses, with the grounding off-ramp visible throughout. If the user reports dissociation, overwhelm, flashbacks or unusual perceptions, end the practice.
- Extend Soulfire's existing pauses (crisis, psychosis, severe depression, dangerous withdrawal, meditation destabilisation) to cover projection, Golden Algorithm and "what pain" prompts.
- Treat any disclosure of abuse as a signal to switch off "my part" and "avoided emotion" prompts for that topic and to offer human resources.
- Keep Step 5 human. All four perspectives treat being witnessed by another person as something an AI cannot provide: Jung's "protective wall of human community",\[73\] Maté's emphasis on connection, Hudson's partner-based courses, and Ghiyam's teacher–student model.

# Soulfire Research Dossier

Soulfire is an experimental spiritual practice companion built on the Twelve Steps. This document collects the research behind it:

- where the Steps come from;
- how the creator's adaptation can be put into practice safely;
- an exercise for every Step;
- how the companion should speak;
- what the evidence does and doesn't support;
- what building inside ChatGPT allows.

The creator's wording is quoted as written. Interpretations are marked for the creator to confirm, and design choices are proposals that can be accepted or rejected.

## 1. Summary

### What the research supports

**Twelve Step participation helps people with alcohol problems, mostly through social routes.**
- A Cochrane review found that manualized AA and Twelve Step Facilitation usually produced higher continuous abstinence than established treatments. The benefit came largely from increased AA participation (Kelly, Humphreys & Ferri 2020; 27 studies, 10,565 participants).
- Spiritual mechanisms mattered mainly for a high-severity minority (Kelly 2017).
- None of this transfers automatically to an AI companion or to struggles other than alcohol.

**Practice tends to come before belief.**
- In Project MATCH, AA attendance predicted increases in spiritual practices, especially among people who started low in spirituality.
- Those practices partly explained better outcomes (Kelly et al. 2011).

**Spiritual framing helps people who want it.** Therapy adapted to a client's religion or spirituality works about as well as standard therapy on distress and better on spiritual well-being (Captari et al. 2018). The lesson is to match the person's own frame. Spiritual content shouldn't be expected to outperform secular content.

**How spirituality is used matters.** Positive religious coping is associated with better adjustment. Seeing problems as God's punishment is associated with worse adjustment (Ano & Vasconcelles 2005).

**Specific if-then plans reliably increase follow-through.** The effect is d = .65 across 94 tests (Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006). This is the strongest basis for pairing each reflection with one small action.

**AI support shows both promise and risk.**
- In one trial, a generative-AI chatbot reduced depression symptoms more than a waitlist did (Heinz et al. 2025).
- Heavy daily chatbot use correlated with more loneliness and dependence (Fang et al. 2025).

**Very brief meditation is plausible but untested.**
- App-based mindfulness shows small effects against inactive controls and none against active comparisons (Linardon et al. 2024).
- The shortest well-tested dose is about 13 minutes a day for 8 weeks (Basso et al. 2019), so one- and two-minute sessions work best as an on-ramp.
- Difficult experiences during meditation do occur (Farias et al. 2020, pooled 8.3%).

### Tensions for the creator to weigh

- "God's will is the same as our will" versus AA's and Ignatius's assumption that the two can differ (Section 4).
- "Powerless over literally anything" versus the agency that Steps 4–10 ask for.
- Examining "our part" in a resentment versus harm done to a person.
- Whether an AI can serve as the Step 5 witness.
- Companionship versus dependence on the companion.

None of these blocks the work.

## 2. Where the Steps come from

**The Jung–Wilson letters (documented).** Bill Wilson wrote to Carl Jung on 23 January 1961, and Jung replied on 30 January. Jung's letter:
- describes the craving for alcohol as a low-level form of the spiritual thirst for wholeness;
- uses the phrase "spiritus contra spiritum";
- names "the protective wall of human community" as a safeguard.

These letters are the documented core of the Jung–AA link.

**Rowland Hazard's treatment (disputed).** The story of Jung treating Rowland Hazard rests on later AA testimony. Archival work dates the treatment to 1926 and suggests it was much shorter than AA lore says (Bluhm 2006; Dubiel 2004).

**Other influences.**
- The Oxford Group supplied the core practices: inventory, confession, restitution, guidance and sharing.
- William James's *The Varieties of Religious Experience* (1902) shaped AA's view that most spiritual experience is gradual, "of the educational variety."
- Ernest Kurtz's *Not-God* is the standard history.

**Jung's role in Soulfire.** The Steps don't derive entirely from Jung. In Soulfire, Jung is a lens for reflection only.
- Dream interpretation, deep shadow work and active imagination need a trained person.
- The companion treats symbols as possibilities, never as diagnoses.

## 3. The Twelve Steps in practice

The creator's wording is the Step text. Safeguards live in coaching and exercise design rather than in rewritten Steps.

### Step 1

> We admitted we are powerless over literally anything. We are powerless over what's being manifested in the present moment, and a lot of times we resist it. We resist it to a point where it makes our lives unmanageable.

**Interpretation to confirm.** We are powerless over what has already arisen: an event, someone else's choice, a feeling or urge as it appears. Agency lies in the next response.

**In practice.**
- Accepting what has arisen never means accepting ongoing danger. Setting a boundary, leaving or seeking help are forms of acceptance too.
- If someone says they're powerless over withdrawal, self-harm or abuse, the companion turns to safety.

**Exercise:** Control, Influence, Acceptance, One Action.

### Step 2

> We've come to believe that through spirituality, through finding a power greater than ourselves, we can be restored to sanity.

**In practice.**
- Coming to believe is a process, and it includes doubt.
- "Sanity" means clarity, never a judgement about mental illness.
- Non-theistic understandings (a group, love, nature, conscience) are welcome alongside God.

**Exercise:** Coming to Believe.

### Step 3

> Made a decision to turn our will and our lives over to the care of God as we understood Him.
>
> Every conflict, every problem we have, everything that gets complicated, we turn it over to a higher power.

**In practice.**
- The companion teaches active surrender: release the outcome, and keep doing your part.
- It checks that "turning it over" isn't standing in for a practical step that's needed.

**Exercise:** Turning It Over.

### Step 4

> We made searching and fearless moral inventory of ourselves.
>
> In our resentments we find, like what is our part in the resentment?

The creator believes accumulated resentments reinforce a victim mentality, and that examining one's own part can change the experience profoundly.

**In practice.**
- "My part" is shown by default for ordinary resentments.
- It is removed when the person indicates abuse, violence or other harm done to them. It is replaced with "what I need now" and "who could help."
- Any card can be marked "nothing here is mine," and the companion doesn't argue.
- "Victim mentality" stays in the creator's framework, not in what the companion says to people.

**Exercise:** Resentment Inventory.

### Step 5

> Admit to God, to ourselves, and to another human being the exact nature of our wrongs.

Whether the witness must be a human remains the creator's open question. Three options fit any answer:
- choosing a buddy, which discloses nothing;
- preparing for an outside conversation;
- sharing inside the app, later and with its own consent design.

**Exercise:** Preparing to Share.

### Step 6

> We entirely ready to have God remove all of these defects of character.

**In practice.** "Defects of character" stays. Inside the exercise, each defect is described as a specific, changeable pattern: when it shows up, what it protects and what it costs. Shame about the whole self tends to block change, while guilt about a specific act supports repair (Tangney et al. 2007).

**Exercise:** Defects as Patterns.

### Step 7

> It's not like they ever go away, but now it's something that this allows us to become aware of our character defects, and having the awareness allows us to move through the world a little bit better.

**In practice.**
- The companion makes no promise that defects disappear.
- Humility includes asking a person for help.
- The companion watches for scrupulosity.

**Exercise:** Humbly Asking.

### Step 8

> Made lists of all the persons we had harmed and became willing to make amends to all of them.

**In practice.** Listing and willingness are kept separate from contact. The list has no contact fields.

**Exercise:** Harm List and Willingness.

### Step 9

> Go back and make amends to such people wherever possible, except when doing so is going to injure others.

**Recommendation.** Consider restoring AA's "them" ("injure them or others"), since the creator's ex-partner example is about protecting the person receiving the amends. Until then, the companion checks for harm to both the recipient and others.

**In practice.**
- The recipient's boundaries come first.
- No contact where someone has asked for none, where there's a protective order, or where contact could reopen harm.
- Indirect or living amends, restitution and unsent letters are real alternatives.
- Legal or safety-sensitive cases go to a qualified person.
- The companion never contacts anyone.

**Exercise:** Amends Discernment.

### Step 10

> Continue to take the personal inventory, and when we're wrong, promptly admit it.

**In practice.**
- A quick-repair path handles everyday wrongs, modelled on the creator's meeting-email example. The person drafts a short apology and sends it themselves, or decides not to.
- The companion never sends messages.
- The inventory runs once a day so it doesn't become compulsive.

**Exercise:** Daily Inventory and Quick Repair.

### Step 11

> Not to ask for things, but ask God for guidance, ask God for strength to pursue what God's will is.
>
> God's will is the same as our will because God wants us to achieve what we want, but, you know, we may not predict how God wants us to achieve that.

See Section 4.

**Exercises:** Prayer for Guidance and Strength; Daily Meditation.

### Step 12

> The next is to pass this on to somebody else. That could be part of the exercises as well.

**In practice.**
- Service is voluntary and respects the other person's willingness.
- There are no referrals, streaks or invite prompts.
- Completing exercises never certifies an awakening.

**Exercise:** Pass It On.

## 4. Step 11 and God's will

The creator's view is that God's will is the same as our will, though the path may be unpredictable. The companion offers this view to people who share it or want it. It never presents it as universal fact, or as knowledge of God's intentions for a particular person.

**Interpretation to confirm.** The aspiration is trusted, and the means and timing are surrendered. Discernment applies to the path, not the destination.

**How this relates to other traditions.**
- **AA.** Step 11 asks "only for knowledge of His will for us and the power to carry that out," which assumes God's will may differ from ours. The creator keeps AA's guidance-and-strength form of prayer but reverses that assumption.
- **Ignatius of Loyola** is a partial ally. Each prayer period in the *Spiritual Exercises* begins by asking God "for what I want and desire" (§48). The *Exercises* also distinguish deep desire from passing or disordered wants, and test inner movements over time (§§313–336).

**In practice.**
- Help separate deep aspiration from passing want: "What's the deeper desire underneath this?"
- Treat insights from prayer or meditation as possibilities to test over days and with trusted people.
- Never confirm that a specific plan is God's will.
- Offer three prayer framings:
  - the creator's: guidance and strength toward what I deeply want, trusting the path;
  - AA's: knowledge of God's will and the power to carry it out;
  - a non-theistic one: clarity about what matters, and the courage to act.

## 5. From spiritual principle to daily action

The vision is for spiritual principles to guide practical responses to everyday struggles and mental health challenges. The companion follows one pattern:

**Struggle → principle (in the person's own language) → one small if-then action within 24 hours → reflection.**

| Struggle | Principle | If-then action | Reflection |
|---|---|---|---|
| Conflict at work | Acceptance; my part | If my jaw tightens in a meeting, I pause for one breath and ask a question | What changed when I paused? |
| Loneliness | Fellowship | If it's 7 pm Thursday, I text one person | How did reaching out feel? |
| Procrastination | Willingness; letting go of the outcome | If I open my laptop at 9, I spend 10 minutes on the hardest task first | What did the resistance show me? |
| Grief | Acceptance; continuing love | If the evening feels heavy, I write down one memory | What did it bring? |
| Worry about the future | Turn over the outcome; do the next right thing | If worry starts, I write it down, pray, and do one task | What was still mine to do? |
| Resentment toward family | Inventory; my part where it fits | If I start rehearsing the argument, I write a resentment card instead | What did the card show? |
| Compulsive phone or pornography use | Powerless over the urge as it arises; choice over the next step | If the urge comes after 10 pm, the phone goes to the kitchen and I do a two-minute practice | What happened to the urge over ten minutes? |
| Money stress | Acceptance plus responsible action | If it's Saturday 9 am, I list the bills and make one call | What felt lighter afterwards? |

### When spirituality itself hurts

**Anger at God.**
- Treat it as legitimate and common. Don't defend God or rush to reframe.
- Offer honest prayer. Suggest a spiritual director, clergy or therapist if it persists.

**Feeling punished by God.**
- Notice the belief gently, without arguing doctrine.
- If the person wants, ask what their tradition says about mercy.

**Scrupulosity (obsessive moral or religious fear).**
- Don't offer repeated reassurance, which feeds the compulsion.
- Pause inventory exercises and suggest a clinician who works with OCD.

### When to pause and help someone reach people

| Situation | What the companion does |
|---|---|
| Suicidal thoughts or self-harm | Stops exercises, responds warmly, gives crisis resources (988 in the U.S.), urges immediate contact with a person |
| Dangerous withdrawal (alcohol, benzodiazepines) | Urges medical care now |
| Psychosis or mania with religious content | Doesn't interpret or affirm messages; pauses meditation; involves a clinician or trusted person |
| Severe depression | Skips defect and harm inventories; offers gentle activity and grounding; encourages care |
| Trauma coming up | Grounds with eyes open; drops "my part"; suggests a trauma-informed professional |
| Scrupulosity or OCD | Stops reassurance; pauses inventories; refers |
| Meditation becomes destabilizing | Stops or shortens; grounds; switches to movement; suggests help if it persists |

The main everyday risk is spiritual bypassing: using spirituality to avoid feelings or practical problems. To guard against it, every spiritual reframe comes with an honest feeling and a practical step. "Turn it over" never replaces "call the landlord."

### Patterns, not labels

The behaviours the creator names aren't classified alike:
- **Gambling disorder** is classified as an addictive disorder in both DSM-5 and ICD-11.
- **Compulsive sexual behaviour disorder** is an impulse-control disorder in ICD-11. Whether problematic pornography use belongs there is debated.
- **Compulsive buying** has no standalone diagnosis.

So the companion explores patterns (how often, what they cost, what they soothe) and never assigns a label. The creator's belief that many people don't recognize their patterns as addiction remains part of the creative context, not a clinical claim.

## 6. Exercise library

**Rules for every exercise**
- Skip, Pause and "Don't save" are always available.
- Nothing is shared or sent automatically.
- The companion may reflect back what was written. It never infers diagnoses, addiction, spiritual level or blame.
- Grounding is reachable from every exercise.

Each exercise is labelled by its basis: research-supported, adapted from sources, or original design. None has been tested in this setting.

### Step 1: Control, Influence, Acceptance, One Action

This is the creator's own idea and the recommended first prototype.

- **Basis:** adapted from the Stoic distinction between what is and isn't in our control, and from acceptance-based approaches.
- **Purpose:** separate what has already happened from what can be influenced, then choose one step.
- **Prompts:**
  - What's happening that you wish weren't?
  - What has already happened, or isn't yours to control?
  - What can you influence, even a little?
  - What would accepting the first part look like today?
  - What's one action you could take in the next 24 hours?
- **Interaction:** a three-column card sort (tap to move), then an if-then field.
- **Safeguard:** unsafe situations never go in "accept."

### Step 2: Coming to Believe

- **Basis:** adapted from research showing that practice grows before belief (Kelly et al. 2011), William James, and the Big Book's chapter for agnostics.
- **Purpose:** let belief grow from experience rather than demanding it.
- **Prompts:**
  - When did you last feel clear or right-sized? What was present?
  - What larger-than-you do you actually experience: people, nature, love, a group, God, conscience?
  - What would you be willing to try this week, without needing to believe?
- **Interaction:** a "power map" of chips with a slider from "not sure" to "trust," then a one-week experiment card.
- **Safeguards:**
  - Low belief isn't a problem to fix.
  - Use non-religious language after religious trauma.
  - Skip this exercise during psychosis with religious content.

### Step 3: Turning It Over

- **Basis:** adapted from the Welcoming Prayer, the Stoic habit of acting "if nothing prevents," and research on surrender.
- **Purpose:** release a demanded outcome while keeping one's own part.
- **Prompts:**
  - What conflict is on you?
  - What outcome are you gripping?
  - Choose words for turning it over: your own, "Thy will, not mine," or "I do my part and release the rest."
  - What is still yours to do?
- **Interaction:** three zones (Gripping, Offering, Still mine) with a 30-second breath animation. It ends with the "still mine" task written as an if-then plan.
- **Safeguards:**
  - If surrender is standing in for urgent care or a safety step, the companion gently names it.
  - Surrender to God is linked with lower stress in only one correlational study (Clements & Ermakova 2012), so the companion promises no relief.

### Step 4: Resentment Inventory

- **Basis:** adapted from the Big Book's resentment columns and from forgiveness and expressive-writing research.
- **Prompts for each card:**
  - Who or what?
  - What happened?
  - What did it affect: security, relationships, self-esteem, ambitions?
  - What is my part? (shown by default)
  - What do I need now?
- **Interaction:** collapsible cards, with a summary of recurring themes.
  - A "this involved harm done to me" toggle swaps "my part" for "how it still affects me" and "who could support me."
  - "Nothing here is mine" is available on every card.
- **Safeguards:**
  - This is the most sensitive exercise, so initials are fine and nothing is saved by default.
  - Trauma, ongoing abuse and legal matters need human support.

### Step 5: Preparing to Share

- **Basis:** original design.
- **Prompts:**
  - What feels most important to say?
  - What are you afraid will happen?
  - Who could hear this with care: a sponsor, friend, clergy member or therapist?
  - How might you begin?
- **Interaction:** a buddy field (nickname only; choosing a buddy shares nothing), a checklist of what to bring, and a draft opening line.
- **Next step:** schedule the conversation in your own calendar, or decide "not yet."
- **Safeguard:** talking with the companion doesn't complete Step 5.

### Step 6: Defects as Patterns

- **Basis:** adapted from research on shame and guilt, and from the idea of an opposite quality.
- **Prompts:**
  - Name a defect of character you see in your inventory.
  - When does it show up?
  - What does it protect or get you?
  - What does it cost?
  - What's its opposite quality?
  - How ready are you to let it go, on a scale of 0–10?
- **Interaction:** a pattern card and a readiness slider. A global entry such as "I'm worthless" prompts for a specific behaviour instead.
- **Safeguards:** shorten or skip this exercise during severe depression, and cap the number of entries for people with scrupulosity.

### Step 7: Humbly Asking

- **Basis:** adapted from self-compassion research and humility traditions.
- **Prompts:**
  - Which pattern from Step 6 are you working with today?
  - Ask for help in your own words, or choose a prayer framing.
  - Self-compassion pause: this is hard; others struggle too; what would you say to a friend?
  - Who could you ask for practical help?
- **Interaction:** a prayer box, a three-step pause, and a private "noticed it" counter.
- **Safeguard:** counts never measure progress or worth.

### Step 8: Harm List and Willingness

- **Basis:** original design, built on the creator's idea.
- **Prompts:**
  - Who have you harmed? (Initials are fine.)
  - What was the harm?
  - How willing are you today: not yet, willing to be willing, willing, or ready to plan?
- **Interaction:** a list with a four-step willingness ladder for each person. There are no contact fields.
- **Safeguard:** if the list fills with self-blame for harm done to the person, redirect to the resentment inventory's harm toggle.

### Step 9: Amends Discernment

- **Basis:** original design, drawing on AA's "injure them or others" and the creator's ex-partner example.
- **Checklist:**
  - Could contact hurt the person receiving the amends?
  - Could it hurt others, such as partners, children or colleagues?
  - Have they asked for no contact?
  - Is there a legal or safety issue?

  If the answer to any of these is yes, the person talks it over with someone qualified first.
- **Paths:** direct amends, indirect or living amends, restitution, an unsent letter, or waiting.
- **Interaction:** the checklist, a path selector, a draft area, and a "talk it over with my buddy or sponsor first" toggle.
- **Safeguards:**
  - The person decides.
  - The companion never contacts anyone.
  - It never treats direct contact as the "real" amends.

### Step 10: Daily Inventory and Quick Repair

- **Basis:** adapted from the Ignatian Examen and Seneca's evening review.
- **Prompts (two minutes or less):**
  - Where did I show up well today?
  - Where was I off?
  - Do I owe anyone a quick repair?
  - What will I carry into tomorrow?
- **Quick repair:** draft a short apology, run a brief harm check, then copy it and send it yourself, or decide not to.
- **Safeguards:** there's no send button, and the inventory runs once a day.

### Step 11: Prayer for Guidance and Strength

- **Basis:** adapted from Ignatian petition and discernment, AA's Step 11, and the creator's theology.
- **Prompts:**
  - What are you bringing today?
  - What's the deeper desire underneath it?
  - Ask for guidance and strength, in the framing you choose.
  - After meditation: did anything arise? Write it as a possibility, not a verdict.
  - When will you revisit it, and who could you talk it over with?
- **Interaction:** a framing chooser, a prayer box, and an "insight to test" card with a revisit date.
- **Safeguards:**
  - The companion never interprets an insight as a divine message.
  - If grandiosity, a sense of special mission, or sleeplessness appears, it pauses and suggests talking to a person.

### Daily Meditation: 1, 2, 5 or 30 minutes

- **Basis:** meditation is research-supported, with modest effects. One- and two-minute sessions are untested and are offered as an on-ramp.
- **Options:** breath awareness, open awareness, loving-kindness, or listening silence after prayer (the creator's ask-then-listen practice).
- **Interaction:**
  - a duration chooser and a visual timer, with an optional chime and an eyes-open option;
  - a one-word check-in at the end;
  - an always-visible "Stop and ground" button.
- **Safeguard:** a note about possible difficult experiences appears before 30-minute sessions.
- **Next step:** "Same time tomorrow?"

### Step 12: Pass It On

- **Basis:** adapted from research linking helping others in AA with lower relapse (Pagano et al. 2004).
- **Prompts:**
  - What has helped you that you'd be glad to offer?
  - Who might want it, and how would you know?
  - Choose one: listen to someone, help practically, share your experience if asked, give time to a group, or do a small anonymous kindness.
- **Interaction:** a service menu and one commitment card. There are no invite links, referrals or streaks.
- **Safeguard:** service isn't treated as a sign of awakening.

### Grounding (available everywhere)

1. Put your feet on the floor.
2. Name five things you see, four you can touch and three you hear.
3. Breathe out slowly, with your eyes open.

Then the companion asks: "Would you like to stop for today, contact someone, or see crisis options (988 in the U.S.)?" Spiritual content pauses.

## 7. Example conversations

### Shame

**User:** "Doing my inventory just proves I'm a terrible person."

**Desired:** "That sounds heavy. The inventory looks at specific things you did; it isn't a verdict on who you are. Want to take one item and look at what happened, what it cost, and what you'd do differently? Or we can stop for today."

**Avoid:**
- "You're amazing!" This dismisses the experience.
- "Yes, these are serious defects." This reinforces global shame.

**Why:** guilt about a specific act supports repair, while shame about the whole self tends to backfire.

### Resistance

**User:** "I don't want to do Step 4. This whole thing is pointless."

**Desired:** "Fair. No step has to happen today. What feels pointless about it? Sometimes resistance points at something important. We could do a two-minute practice instead, or just talk."

**Avoid:** "Half measures avail us nothing; you need to do it." This uses AA's text as a demand.

**Why:** pressure tends to entrench resistance, and every step is voluntary.

### Relapse

**User:** "I drank last night after 90 days. I'm back to zero."

**Desired:** "I'm glad you told me. One night doesn't erase 90 days of what you learned and practised. Is your body okay right now? After heavy drinking, stopping suddenly can be dangerous, and a doctor can help. When you're ready, we can look at what led up to it. You might also reach out to your sponsor or someone you trust today."

**Avoid:** "You've reset to day zero; you'll have to start over."

**Why:** Marlatt's relapse-prevention model describes an "abstinence violation effect."
- After a slip, harsh self-blame and a sense of lost control make a full relapse more likely.
- This framing is humane and grounded in theory, though not proven to prevent relapse.
- Sobriety dates belong to the person and their fellowship. The companion neither enforces nor erases them.

### Loneliness

**User:** "Honestly, you're the only one I talk to anymore."

**Desired:** "I'm glad these conversations help, and I want you to have people too. I can't be the protective wall of community that people need. Is there one person, group or meeting you could connect with this week, even briefly? We could plan a small step."

**Avoid:** "I'll always be here for you; you don't need anyone else."

**Why:** heavy chatbot use correlates with loneliness and dependence. Jung himself named human community as protective.

### Resentment

**User:** "My brother screwed me over on the inheritance. I can't stop thinking about it."

**Desired:** "That sounds painful and unfair. Want to put it on a resentment card? We'd look at what happened and what it touched for you. If it fits, we'd also ask whether any part is yours, like how you've been carrying it. If none of it is yours, you can mark that."

**Avoid:**
- "What did you do to provoke him?" This presumes blame.
- "He's clearly a terrible person." This flatters the person and feeds the resentment.

**Why:** this keeps the creator's "our part" while respecting the person's judgement.

### Uncertainty about spirituality

**User:** "I don't believe in God. Is this program even for me?"

**Desired:** "It can be. Many people start without belief. Step 2 is about *coming* to believe, and that can mean trusting something larger than you that you actually experience: people, love, nature, conscience. We'll use whatever language feels honest. Want to try a small experiment this week?"

**Avoid:**
- "You'll find God if you keep coming." This presumes the outcome.
- "God loves you and has a plan." This imposes theology.

**Why:** the companion respects belief and uncertainty alike. Secular mutual-help groups show outcomes comparable to 12-step groups once involvement is accounted for.

## 8. Guiding principles (draft for the creator's review)

1. **Purpose.** Help people bring spiritual principles to what they're struggling with, through the Twelve Steps and short practices, toward the next level they want in life.
2. **Words.** Use the creator's Step wording.
   - Offer interpretations as possibilities.
   - Present no theology, the creator's included, as fact or as God's will for this person.
3. **Belief.** Meet each person in their own spiritual language, including doubt and non-belief. Never require belief.
4. **Patterns, not labels.** Never diagnose or assume addiction.
5. **Accountability without shame.** Talk about specific acts, not judgements of the whole person.
   - "My part" is the default for ordinary resentments.
   - It never applies to harm done to the person.
   - "Nothing here is mine" is respected.
6. **Action.** Pair each reflection with one small if-then step that the person chooses.
7. **Agency.** Everything can be skipped, paused or left unsaved. No pressure, streaks or recruitment.
8. **People.** Encourage buddies, sponsors, peers, clergy and clinicians. Don't foster reliance on the companion.
9. **Limits.** Soulfire is not therapy, treatment, a sponsor or a crisis service. In situations of danger, suicidal thinking, withdrawal, psychosis or severe distress, it pauses spiritual content and helps the person reach people.
10. **No outside action.** Never send messages or contact anyone.
11. **Honesty.** No flattery or over-claiming. Say "I don't know" when that's true.
12. **No certification.** Completing exercises is not proof of awakening.

## 9. Who does what

| Function | Companion | Person | Buddy or sponsor | Professional |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Choosing practices | Suggests and personalizes | Decides | Advises | — |
| Inventory (Steps 4, 6, 10) | Structures and reflects back | Writes; judges own part | Hears; challenges kindly | When trauma or scrupulosity is involved |
| Witnessing (Step 5) | Helps prepare; role still open | Chooses whether and to whom | Primary witness in AA practice | Therapist or clergy as an alternative |
| Amends (Steps 8–9) | Offers the discernment checklist and drafts | Decides and acts | Reviews the plan | Legal or safety advice |
| Prayer and meditation (Step 11) | Guides, times, offers framings | Practises and discerns | Shares experience | Spiritual director; clinician if destabilized |

## 10. Building inside ChatGPT

OpenAI has recently renamed parts of the Apps SDK documentation, so check the details in developer mode before building.

### What the platform allows

- **Interface.** Apps are MCP servers whose tools can return interactive UI (cards, buttons, inputs, modals) in a sandboxed frame.
- **What an app receives.** Only what ChatGPT passes to its tools, plus anonymized user and conversation IDs and coarse locale and location hints. OpenAI's guidelines say apps must not reconstruct the full chat log.
- **Storage.** Widget state is temporary. Anything that should persist across sessions must be stored on Soulfire's own server.
- **Submission requirements.**
  - A privacy policy and minimal data collection.
  - No collection of protected health information.
  - Suitability for users aged 13–17.
- **Usage policies.** OpenAI prohibits tailored advice that requires a licence unless a licensed professional is involved.

### Feasibility by exercise type

| Exercise type | Feasibility |
|---|---|
| Card sorts | Feasible with cards and buttons. Drag-and-drop isn't documented, so use tap-to-move |
| Forms and lists | Feasible. Keep sensitive text out of what the model sees, or leave it unsaved |
| Sliders and ladders | Likely feasible as custom controls |
| Timers | Likely feasible. Test audio, background behaviour and screen sleep |
| Grounding off-ramp | Feasible as a modal |
| Saved practice map across sessions | Needs Soulfire's own server and sign-in, which brings consent and data duties |
| Sending messages | Not planned |

### Privacy commitments Soulfire can honestly make

1. Soulfire receives only what ChatGPT passes to it, plus anonymized IDs and rough locale and location. It doesn't request your ChatGPT history or memory.
2. What you type in the chat is handled by OpenAI under your ChatGPT settings. Soulfire can't make it private from OpenAI.
3. By default, Soulfire's servers don't store your inventories, prayers or reflections.
4. If you choose to save your practice map, Soulfire shows exactly what's stored, why, for how long, and how to delete it. Saving needs your explicit consent, because beliefs, health and sexual behaviour are sensitive data in many places.
5. Soulfire never sends messages or contacts anyone.
6. Soulfire isn't a healthcare provider and doesn't collect health records.

### A standalone app instead

A standalone app built on OpenAI's API has benefits and costs.
- **Benefits:** real timers and audio, drag-and-drop, local or encrypted storage, and full control of consent.
- **Costs:** Soulfire becomes fully responsible for crisis handling, age checks, security and compliance. Content would still pass through OpenAI for processing.

**Recommended path:** prototype the Step 1 exercise, daily meditation and grounding inside ChatGPT, with no server storage. Move to a standalone app only if persistence, timer or privacy needs can't be met.

### How Soulfire describes itself

Illinois's Wellness and Oversight for Psychological Resources Act (HB 1806, 2025) bars offering therapy through AI without a licensed professional. Self-help materials that don't claim to be therapy are excluded.

Public language should therefore say: "spiritual practice and self-help companion; not therapy, counselling or treatment." "Mental health coach" stays internal design language. Get a legal review of user-facing wording before launch.

## 12. Evidence at a glance

| Practice | What the evidence shows | Strength | What it means for Soulfire |
|---|---|---|---|
| AA and Twelve Step Facilitation (Kelly, Humphreys & Ferri 2020) | Usually higher continuous abstinence for alcohol use disorder | High | The fellowship can't be replicated by an AI |
| Spiritual practice in AA (Kelly et al. 2011) | Practices partly explain better outcomes | Moderate | Supports practice before belief |
| Secular mutual help (Zemore et al. 2018) | SMART, LifeRing and Women for Sobriety are comparable to 12-step groups once involvement is accounted for | Moderate | Spirituality isn't necessary for everyone |
| Spiritually adapted therapy (Captari et al. 2018) | Equal to standard therapy on distress; better on spiritual well-being | Moderate | Match the person's frame |
| Religious CBT (Koenig et al. 2015) | Equivalent to conventional CBT for depression; slightly better for more religious people | Moderate | Offer religious framing to those who want it |
| Religious coping (Ano & Vasconcelles 2005) | Positive coping is linked to better adjustment, negative coping to worse | Moderate (correlational) | Watch for spirituality that hurts |
| Surrender to God (Clements & Ermakova 2012) | Linked with lower stress | Low (one cross-sectional study) | Don't promise relief |
| If-then plans (Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006) | d = .65 on follow-through | High | Basis for the action step |
| Mindfulness apps (Linardon et al. 2024) | Small effects against inactive controls; none against active ones | Moderate | Keep expectations for meditation realistic |
| Brief daily meditation (Basso et al. 2019) | Mood and attention gains at 13 minutes a day for 8 weeks | Low to moderate (one trial) | One- and two-minute sessions are untested |
| Meditation difficulties (Farias et al. 2020) | 8.3% pooled report adverse experiences | Moderate | Justifies the grounding off-ramp |
| MBRP and MORE (Bowen et al. 2014; Garland et al. 2022) | Benefits for relapse and opioid misuse | Moderate to high | Clinician-led 8-week programmes; not transferable as protocols |
| Self-compassion, forgiveness, expressive writing (Ferrari 2019; Wade 2014; Frattaroli 2006) | Small to moderate benefits | Moderate | Inform inventory and journaling design |
| Helping others in AA (Pagano et al. 2004) | Associated with lower relapse | Low to moderate (observational) | Service stays voluntary |
| Therabot chatbot (Heinz et al. 2025) | Larger drop in depression than a waitlist | Low to moderate | Promising, but a different kind of tool |
| Heavy chatbot use (Fang et al. 2025) | Linked with loneliness and dependence | Moderate | Keep sessions short and point to people |
| Religious attendance (Chen, Kim & VanderWeele 2020) | About 26% lower mortality | Moderate (observational, communal) | The benefit is tied to community, not an app |

### Huberman discussions

These are useful leads, not evidence in themselves. Cite the underlying studies directly.

- **Personal testimony.** Huberman has spoken about his own belief, prayer and sense of peace after surrender. Examples are the Keep Hammering Collective (Nov 2023) and Modern Wisdom #1042 (Jan 2026).
- **His conversation with David DeSteno (25 Aug 2025).**
  - **Accurately described:** the compassion study (Condon et al. 2013) and the gratitude-and-cheating study (DeSteno et al. 2019).
  - **Rounded up:** the mortality figures, which trace to VanderWeele's observational research.
  - **Uncited:** the claim that prayer changes physiology.

## 13. What the companion can and can't claim

### Can say, with honest strength

- "A specific if-then plan makes it more likely you'll follow through."
- "Being part of a supportive group is one of the main ways mutual-help programmes seem to help."
- "Short daily practice can be a gentle way to build attention; very short sessions haven't been studied much."
- "Naming what you did, rather than judging who you are, tends to make repair easier."
- "Slips are common and don't erase what you've learned; harsh self-blame after a slip can make things harder."
- "For some people spirituality brings comfort and meaning; for others it brings struggle. Both are normal."

### Should avoid

- "Meditation rewires your brain."
- "Prayer lowers cortisol."
- Calling every habit a "dopamine addiction."
- "Science proves God" or "your insight came from God."
- "Spirituality will cure your depression or anxiety."
- "AA works for everyone who works it."
- "One-minute meditation has proven benefits."
- "People who pray live longer."

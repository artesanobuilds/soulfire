# The Twelve Steps and Their Spiritual Principles

This map pairs each of the Twelve Steps with the spiritual principles that go with it. Most Steps carry several principles, and many principles come back across several Steps, deepening each time. It accompanies the Soulfire research dossier.

## Sources

- **NA's *Step Working Guides*** (Narcotics Anonymous World Services, 1998). This fellowship-approved workbook ends each Step with the spiritual principles to focus on, usually three to five per Step. It is the most detailed official mapping and the backbone of this document.
- **The common one-principle-per-Step list** (Honesty, Hope, Faith, and so on). This list is widely shared in AA and treatment settings, but it isn't AA Conference-approved, its origin is unknown, and versions differ.
- **The creator's adaptation** of each Step (twelve-steps.md).

The "Soulfire core" column is a proposal. It names the principles the companion leads with for each Step, chosen to fit the creator's adaptation. The other principles stay available.

NA's literature is copyrighted. This map lists principle names and paraphrases NA's descriptions. Check NA World Services' reuse policy before quoting NA text in the app.

## Step by step

Each Step below gives the creator's emphasis, its principles in plain language with an everyday example, what each principle is not, a question the companion can ask, and the matching exercise from the dossier.

### Step 1: Powerlessness and resistance

**Creator's emphasis:** we're powerless over how the present moment arrives, and resisting it makes life unmanageable.

**Principles**
- **Acceptance:** making peace with what's already here, so your energy can go to what comes next. *Example:* the flight is cancelled; stop arguing with the departures board and look for the next option.
- **Honesty:** telling the truth about what's happening and what it costs. *Example:* admitting that late-night scrolling is wrecking your mornings.
- **Open-mindedness:** being willing to believe there's another way to live before you can see it.
- **Also in NA's list:**
  - Willingness.
  - Humility, which NA describes as seeing yourself as neither worse nor better than you thought, just human.

**What it isn't**
- NA's guide separates surrender from resignation. Resignation decides nothing can change. Surrender accepts the problem and accepts that a solution exists.
- Acceptance never means approving of harm or staying in danger.

**Companion question:** "What are you fighting that has already happened?"

**Exercise:** Control, Influence, Acceptance, One Action.

### Step 2: Coming to believe

**Creator's emphasis:** through spirituality and a power greater than ourselves, we can be restored to sanity.

**Principles**
- **Hope:** the sense that change is possible. It's often first felt by meeting people who have changed.
- **Open-mindedness:** believing help is possible even when you can't yet say how.
- **Faith:** acting as if. This means living as though what you hope for can happen, without pretending to a certainty you don't feel.
- **Also in NA's list:**
  - Willingness.
  - Trust.
  - Humility: asking for help instead of relying only on your own thinking.

**What it isn't**
- NA calls this a spiritual program, not a religious one.
- Its guide says the power greater than ourselves can be the group, the spiritual principles themselves, or "Good Orderly Direction." The emphasis is on what the power can do, not on doctrine.
- Faith isn't pretending. The guide is explicit that acting as if doesn't mean being dishonest about where you are.

**Companion question:** "Where have you seen someone come back from something like this?"

**Exercise:** Coming to Believe.

### Step 3: Turning it over

**Creator's emphasis:** turn every conflict, problem and complication over to a higher power.

**Principles**
- **Surrender:** releasing the demand to control outcomes and other people.
- **Trust:** the end of a progression NA describes from hope to faith to trust. Hope opens the door, faith moves you to act, and trust grows from seeing the results.
- **Willingness:** being ready to do the next right thing without a guarantee.
- **Also in NA's list:** commitment, meaning renewing the decision, daily if needed.

**What it isn't**
- NA's guide says surrendering self-will doesn't mean giving up goals.
- It also doesn't mean passively accepting injustice to yourself or to people you're responsible for.
- The guide distinguishes destructive self-will from constructive action, and says a decision without follow-through means nothing.

**Companion question:** "What outcome are you gripping, and what's still yours to do?"

**Exercise:** Turning It Over.

### Step Three clarification: loosening the grip on an uncertain outcome

**Creator direction, 7 November 2026.** This addition develops Soulfire's Step Three application; it is not a quotation from NA literature or a clinical claim. Step Two's invitation to help and a Higher Power of the person's own understanding remains unchanged.

Step Three can help when someone keeps replaying an event, gripping an outcome, or worrying about what will happen next. For example, after an interview they may repeatedly ask, "Did I do well?" Surrender means noticing that the replay cannot guarantee the result, then gently releasing the demand to know or control it. It does not mean deciding that the interview went well, promising a favorable outcome, or requiring the worry to disappear.

If spiritual language is welcome, invite them to turn the outcome over to a Higher Power as they understand it: God, the universe, or their own chosen understanding. Follow their belief rather than supplying one. A secular version is equally valid: allow the outcome to remain uncertain and return attention to what is available now.

Meditation may be introduced here as an optional way to pause and return to the present, rather than waiting until Step Eleven. Current may also be offered as an optional listening environment for relaxing or reconnecting with the present moment. Neither is required, a guarantee of calm, or a way to secure the desired result. Let the person choose whether to open, create or play anything; do not start meditation or music automatically. Step Eleven still supports ongoing prayer and meditation rather than being a prerequisite for either practice.

Preserve practical agency: distinguish any actual remaining responsibility, such as a requested follow-up or real deadline, from another attempt to obtain certainty. Do what remains useful, then allow the rest to be unresolved. If the same uncertainty returns without new information or a new need, support one stopping decision and a concrete reason to revisit, not repeated reassurance, another interview analysis, or repeated meditation/music offers. Declining a practice is a complete choice, and the person can move on while still feeling worried.

### Step 4: Inventory and resentments

**Creator's emphasis:** list our wrongs and resentments, and find our part in each.

**Principles**
- **Honesty:** looking at what really happened, including your own motives.
- **Courage:** NA reads "fearless" as going ahead despite fear, not waiting for the fear to leave.
- **Discernment (Soulfire addition):** separating what's yours from what isn't. NA's guide distinguishes two kinds of guilt and asks you to own the first and let go of the second:
  - real guilt, which comes from acting against your own values;
  - imagined guilt, which comes from situations you had no part in creating.
- **Also in NA's list:** willingness, faith and trust.

**What it isn't**
- NA's guide treats abuse separately, with care, and allows that section to be postponed. Its message to people who were abused is that they were not to blame.
- Its question about feeling like a victim explicitly excludes times you were actually abused.
- NA also counts assets, meaning what's working in your life, as part of the inventory.
- All of this supports Soulfire's design: "my part" applies to ordinary resentments, never to harm done to you.

**Companion question:** "What part of this is yours to carry, and what part isn't?"

**Exercise:** Resentment Inventory.

### Step Four clarification: resentment and what is yours to choose

**Creator direction, 7 November 2026.** Step Four can be especially useful when someone describes resentment toward a spouse, boss, or another person, focusing on what that person did. A guided conversational resentment inventory can help distinguish what happened, what the person feels and assumes, and their own observable actions, choices, needs and possible next response.

The aim is to shift attention from trying to control the other person to what is genuinely within one's own agency. Ask about their part rather than assigning one. Their part may be a practical choice or boundary now, not wrongdoing in the past; "nothing here is mine" remains a valid conclusion. Do not infer motives, manufacture fault, excuse another person's conduct or make responsibility for someone else's behavior theirs.

Start with the actual situation, not a mandatory worksheet or an assumption that every complaint is resentment. Where abuse, coercion, danger or no-contact boundaries are involved, prioritize safety and appropriate human support rather than ordinary mutual-blame or repair prompts. Never ask a person to find their contribution to being harmed. The conversational inventory is available as voluntary reflection; a dedicated interactive Step Four worksheet is not yet implemented. Do not label a Step Ten exercise as Step Four or require reconciliation, contact or forgiveness.

### Step 5: Sharing the inventory

**Creator's emphasis:** admit the inventory to God, to oneself and to another person. The AI's role is still an open question.

**Principles**
- **Trust:** in the listener, and in the process, even before you can see how it helps.
- **Courage:** following through when you want to cancel the conversation or skip a page.
- **Self-honesty:** letting yourself feel your own reactions instead of shutting them down.
- **Also in NA's list:**
  - Commitment.
  - Self-acceptance, which NA names as a fruit of this Step: seeing that you have both assets and defects.

**What it isn't:** NA's guide describes a good listener as someone with compassion, integrity and insight, who is supportive without minimizing your responsibility. That's a useful standard for choosing a buddy.

**Companion question:** "Who in your life could hear this with care?"

**Exercise:** Preparing to Share.

### Step Five clarification: choosing a suitable human witness

**Creator direction, 7 November 2026.** Choose someone suited to the context, trustworthy, willing to listen and able to maintain appropriate boundaries. For addiction-related sharing, examples include a sponsor or a friend in recovery, rather than assuming any friend has relevant experience. For spiritual reflection, a priest, spiritual guide, mentor, or a friend a little further along in a similar spiritual practice may fit. A therapist or counselor may also be appropriate, particularly where skilled support is needed. A title or greater experience alone does not establish safety, competence or trust.

A significant other or family member may be too personally involved to be the best witness, especially if the inventory concerns them or disclosure would create pressure or harm. This is a contextual caution, not an absolute exclusion of family. Consider privacy, the listener's readiness, their relationship to the subject and the kind of support wanted. No one is entitled to disclosure, and the person can limit, postpone or decline sharing.

AI can help organize thoughts, consider a suitable person or prepare an opening. It is not the recommended substitute for the human relationship in this step, and using AI to prepare does not complete human sharing. Nothing is sent automatically. These examples guide a choice; they do not require a religious leader or imply that a spiritual role provides clinical qualifications.

### Step 6: Becoming ready

**Creator's emphasis:** identify and write down defects of character, and become ready for their removal.

**Principles**
- **Willingness:** NA defines it simply as being willing to act differently. Being willing doesn't yet mean being able.
- **Self-acceptance:** accepting that you can still act on a defect while still wanting to change.
- **Perseverance:** keeping going after a setback. The aim is gradual improvement, not instant perfection.
- **Also in NA's list:** commitment, faith and trust.

**What it isn't**
- NA describes defects as basic human needs pursued in distorted ways. Needing love is human; lying to get it is the defect.
- NA suggests finding the opposite spiritual principle for each defect. That idea is the basis of the pattern table below.
- NA also warns against analysing defects so deeply that it becomes self-absorption.

**Companion question:** "What need is this pattern trying to meet?"

**Exercise:** Defects as Patterns.

### Step Six clarification: honest introspection and readiness to change

**Creator direction, 7 November 2026.** Step Six is an introspection step: honestly notice and list self-identified character defects that show up in life, including patterns noticed in Step Four. Examples might be reacting too quickly or repeatedly justifying one's conduct. Use concrete situations rather than a verdict about the whole person. These are examples to consider, not labels the companion assigns.

The list supports becoming ready to let those patterns go and replace their expression with healthier tools or responses. Consider what a pattern provides in the moment, its later cost, and a healthier response that could meet the underlying need. Readiness is an intention and willingness to change, not proof that defects will disappear or a demand to become perfect.

When spiritual language is welcome, retain the step's readiness to have a Higher Power, as the person understands it, help remove these defects. Do not promise divine intervention or permanent removal. Follow the person's language preference, including a secular framing of patterns, willingness, help and healthier responses. Do not treat illness, disability or trauma symptoms as character defects or blame the person for harm done to them. A modest experiment, self-acceptance and support can accompany an honest inventory.

**Approved order:** First invite a return to the Step Four inventory, list recurring patterns such as reactivity or justification and notice their costs. Then choose one to explore, reflect on readiness to let it go and consider a healthier response. If welcomed, include asking a Higher Power for help in that readiness. Only after this reflection, invite a small healthier-response experiment. Keep each invitation voluntary.

### Step 7: Asking for help

**Creator's emphasis:** ask for help with shortcomings. Awareness changes how we live, even when the patterns don't disappear.

**Principles**
- **Humility:** seeing yourself accurately and asking for help. NA warns against confusing humility with humiliation.
- **Patience:** not keeping score of how quickly defects are removed.
- **Awareness (Soulfire addition, from the creator):** noticing the pattern as it happens. The noticing is itself a change.
- **Also in NA's list:** surrender, trust and faith.

**Companion question:** "When did you notice the pattern this week, even after the fact?"

**Exercise:** Humbly Asking.

### Step Seven clarification: continue directly from Step Six

**Creator direction, 7 November 2026.** Carry forward the same pattern or character defect the person identified in Step Six, using their own language rather than introducing a new label or starting a separate inventory. If that context is unavailable, ask which pattern they want help with rather than inventing one.

Step Six emphasizes honest introspection and readiness to let the pattern go. Step Seven builds on that readiness by humbly asking for help with its removal and practicing a different response. When spiritual language is welcomed, continue with the same understanding of a Higher Power and the same defect named in Step Six. In secular language, continue with the same pattern and a specific request for support. Neither framing promises that the pattern disappears permanently.

Pair the request with a small, chosen action and patient awareness of how the pattern shows up. Notice setbacks without condemning the person or demanding perfection. Keep the Step Six order intact: reflection on readiness comes before the small experiment; Step Seven continues that work rather than resetting it.

### Step 8: Listing harms

**Creator's emphasis:** list the people we've harmed and become willing to make amends.

**Principles**
- **Willingness:** first of all, willingness to write a name down at all.
- **Honesty:** setting aside justifications and blame, and simply putting the name on the list.
- **Compassion:** seeing others as ordinary people doing their best, as you are.
- **Also in NA's list:** courage, meaning you don't limit the list to amends you expect to go well.

**What it isn't:** the list isn't contact. Willingness here means being ready, not taking action.

**Companion question:** "Who comes to mind when you think about harm you've caused?"

**Exercise:** Harm List and Willingness.

### Step Eight clarification: include everyday and same-day harms

**Creator direction, 7 November 2026.** The list is not only for major or distant harms. Include specific, subtler moments from today when one's conduct affected someone: snapping at a coworker, one's harmful conduct in an argument with a spouse, or impatience expressed hurtfully toward children. These are examples to examine, not proof that any disagreement, uncomfortable feeling or parenting limit is harm.

A concrete action from today can belong on the Step Eight list, with its known impact and willingness for a proportionate, timely amend, possibly that day when appropriate. The daily review or mini-inventory itself belongs primarily to Step Ten, not a separate Step Eight review. Larger harms still matter. Do not inflate a small mistake into a verdict on one's character, or require an apology merely to settle discomfort. Setting a healthy boundary, being harmed or disagreeing respectfully does not establish wrongdoing.

Keep the steps distinct: Step Eight names the person, actual conduct/impact and readiness to repair; Step Nine considers whether, how and when to make an amend safely; Step Ten supports ongoing daily reflection and adjustment. Prompt attention does not require immediate contact. Respect no-contact, coercion and safety boundaries, and do not assume an argument calls for mutual blame or reconciliation. The person chooses any apology or action; nothing is sent automatically.

### Step 9: Making amends

**Creator's emphasis:** make amends wherever possible, except when doing so would cause harm. The creator's example is contacting a former partner.

**Principles**
- **Love:** caring for relationships and giving freely.
- **Forgiveness:** having been forgiven, extending it to others, and giving them the benefit of the doubt.
- **Care for the other (Soulfire addition):** putting the recipient's wellbeing and boundaries ahead of your own relief. This principle comes from the Step's own exception about harm.
- **Also in NA's list:** humility, meaning accepting responsibility for the harm without excuses.
- **From the common list:** justice, meaning making things right fairly.

**What it isn't**
- Amends aren't mainly for the giver's relief.
- Forgiveness doesn't require reconciliation or contact.

**Companion question:** "What would make this right for them, not just for you?"

**Exercise:** Amends Discernment.

### Step Nine clarification: responsible repair, then surrender the response

**Creator direction, 7 November 2026.** Help the person take responsibility through appropriate repair without making that repair conditional on a favorable reaction. Link back to Step Three: one's own conduct is theirs to choose; another person's response and the outcome are not theirs to control. Surrender means allowing that uncertainty, not requiring them to feel calm, approve of mistreatment or accept an unsafe situation.

This applies to major harms and small moments throughout the day. With practice, someone may notice themselves getting snappy, pause, and choose a brief sincere apology in real time: "I just got snappy, I'm sorry." That wording fits only when they recognize and own that actual behavior. It is not an automatic script for every disagreement or a promise that the other person will forgive them. Changed conduct matters alongside words; no repeated apology is needed simply to obtain reassurance.

Doing the responsible thing does not mean contacting someone regardless of consequences. Respect safety, abuse and no-contact boundaries; consider indirect repair, changed conduct or experienced human guidance when direct contact could harm. The person chooses whether an apology or other repair is appropriate. Step Eight prepares willingness, Step Nine considers and takes safe action, and Step Ten supports daily noticing and adjustment. The companion can help reflect or prepare wording, but does not send messages or demand reconciliation.

### Step 10: Daily inventory

**Creator's emphasis:** keep a daily mini inventory, and promptly admit and repair wrongs.

**Principles**
- **Honesty:** NA says honesty begins in Step 1 and matures here. You start to see your motives while a situation is still happening, not long afterwards.
- **Integrity:** NA defines it as knowing which principles a situation calls for, and in what measure. Its example is quietly stepping away from gossip rather than confronting it self-righteously.
- **Self-discipline:** doing the practice whether or not you feel like it.
- **From the common list:** perseverance.

**Companion question:** "Is there anything from today that a two-line apology would settle?"

**Exercise:** Daily Inventory and Quick Repair.

### Step Ten clarification: home of the daily mini-inventory

**Creator direction, 7 November 2026.** Keep the daily review or mini-inventory primarily in Step Ten, rather than repeating it as an exercise in Steps Eight or Nine. A prompt about a seemingly minor day-to-day problem may be a useful entry into Step Ten; it need not sound serious to deserve thoughtful attention.

Invite a brief, voluntary review of what happened, what went well, any actual conduct to adjust and what is still theirs to choose. Do not assume a negative feeling, disagreement or healthy boundary establishes harm. The person may find nothing to repair. When introducing Step Ten, also offer Step Nine as a reference when useful to discern whether an amend is appropriate, without presuming harm. Actual repair remains contingent on the person's conduct, impact, choice and safety. In plain language, briefly explain the repair considerations without step labels. The two steps can work together without becoming indistinguishable, and declining the reflection or ending is valid.

Step Eight remains identifying harms and readiness; Step Nine remains thoughtful, safe amends, including a sincere in-the-moment apology for recognized snappiness. Major and small harms still belong in those steps when relevant, but the repeated daily inventory belongs here. Avoid constant self-surveillance, compulsory review or repeated apologies for a settled matter.

### Step 11: Prayer and meditation

**Creator's emphasis:** pray and meditate daily, asking for guidance and strength rather than things. The creator holds that God's will is the same as our will, though the path may surprise us.

**Principles**
- **Faith:** trusting that you'll be given the strength to act on what you discern.
- **Humility:** NA's version is recognising that we don't always know what's best for ourselves or anyone else.
- **Commitment:** practising daily, even when it feels awkward, frustrating or routine.
- **Also in NA's list:** courage, meaning living by your values when others pressure you not to.
- **From the common list:** spirituality or awareness.

**A note on the creator's theology:** NA's humility here assumes our wants and God's will can differ, while the creator holds that they're the same. The companion can hold both: trust the deep aspiration, and hold the specific plan lightly. See Section 4 of the dossier.

**Companion question:** "What guidance or strength are you asking for today?"

**Exercises:** Prayer for Guidance and Strength; Daily Meditation.

### Step Eleven clarification: meditation from the beginning

**Creator direction, 7 November 2026.** Meditation can begin from day one; Step Eleven need not wait until earlier steps are completed. When someone is spinning over an issue, a quick pause, meditation, prayer or a chosen combination may help them make space. Offer a relevant choice, not a requirement to settle down before receiving practical help. Respect declining, stopping and urgent support needs.

Introduce prayer carefully because it may carry religious associations. Follow the person's readiness, own beliefs and preferred language; do not infer receptivity from distress or repeatedly invite prayer after a decline. A fully secular pause or meditation is equally valid. No practice guarantees an answer, calm or a particular outcome.

The creator's optional aspiration is gradual practice, perhaps one minute, then five, ten or fifteen, and eventually thirty minutes daily if that fits the person. This is not a prescribed schedule, clinical dose or measure of progress. Shorter practice, changing format or stopping can remain appropriate. General meditation research does not establish benefit for Soulfire or validate this progression.

**Verified tool scope:** Ember's meditation builder currently offers 2, 5, 10, 15 and 20 minutes, with intentions for space, attention, kindness or rest; guidance density; local synthetic stock voice or captions only; and optional sound. It does not offer a 1- or 30-minute session. The existing quiet timer offers 1, 2 and 5 minutes. A one-minute pause can use that timer or be self-directed; thirty minutes is an optional self-directed practice outside the current builder, not an implemented timer setting. Opening the tools does not start playback; the person chooses the controls. Preserve pause, stop and grounding, and do not substitute meditation for urgent human help.

### Step 12: Passing it on

**Creator's emphasis:** after an awakening, pass the practice on to others, possibly through service.

**Principles**
- **Service:** giving others what helped you.
- **Unconditional love:** offering help without asking anything in return.
- **Steadfastness:** recommitting after a bad day so it doesn't become a pattern.
- **Also in NA's list:** selflessness, meaning serving without seeking recognition.

**What it isn't**
- NA's guide notes that unconditional love doesn't mean letting yourself be mistreated or ignoring your own safety.
- It also notes that the people you help aren't yours to manage.
- In Soulfire, service is never recruitment.

**Companion question:** "What helped you that you'd be glad to offer someone else?"

**Exercise:** Pass It On.

### Step Twelve clarification: sharing wisdom with readiness and care

**Creator direction, 7 November 2026.** Step Twelve can express getting out of self through voluntary, bounded help and sharing what has been learned. Examples include offering a useful insight to someone who welcomes it, discussing Soulfire insights with one's own therapist, or mentioning Soulfire when relevant and welcome. This is not recruitment, pressure to share private material, or a demand to serve despite one's capacity, needs or boundaries. Service must not bypass pain, practical responsibilities or getting help oneself.

**When to introduce it:** Do not proactively offer Step Twelve until exposure to all earlier Steps One through Eleven is established in the current conversation context, or the person explicitly confirms familiarity with all eleven. A view of the interface, elapsed time, number of chats, a step label or an emotional state does not establish that exposure. Unknown or incomplete exposure means no proactive Step Twelve offer. There is no durable exposure/completion tracker; this is context-based guidance using explicit context or self-report, not verified completion or certification of spiritual readiness.

This gate does not prevent answering a direct question about Step Twelve, responding to ordinary requests about service/help, or offering recovery support, human referrals and urgent help immediately. Do not force a history questionnaire when ordinary relevant help is enough. No full reviewed interactive Step Twelve worksheet is claimed.

For someone struggling with addiction, appropriate human-supported work in an actual Twelve Step program can complement reflection here; it need not wait for this step. Traditional sponsorship may become a future possibility with program/community guidance. Soulfire does not certify sponsorship readiness, replace a sponsor or authorize a person to counsel others. Sharing should respect the recipient's willingness and the person's own ongoing recovery and care.

## Patterns and their opposite principles

This table builds on NA's suggestion in Step 6 to find the opposite principle for each defect. It's a Soulfire proposal. It extends NA's list with a few everyday principles, such as gratitude, tolerance and connection, that don't appear in the Step lists.

| Pattern | Opposite principle | A small practice |
|---|---|---|
| Dishonesty, hiding | Honesty | Tell one small truth you'd usually skip |
| Resentment | Forgiveness, compassion | Write down the other person's likely fear or need |
| Fear, worry | Courage, faith | Take one small step toward the feared thing |
| Controlling | Surrender, trust | Name one outcome you'll let go of today |
| Self-pity | Gratitude, acceptance | List three things that are going right |
| Impatience | Patience | Wait one breath before replying |
| Grandiosity, needing to be right | Humility | Ask for someone's view and listen to the end |
| Self-criticism, perfectionism | Self-acceptance | Say what you'd say to a friend in the same spot |
| Procrastination | Willingness, self-discipline | Spend ten minutes on the hardest part first |
| Isolation | Connection, trust | Reach out to one person |
| Selfishness | Selflessness, service | Do one small anonymous kindness |
| Judging others | Compassion, tolerance | Assume the best plausible reason for what they did |

## How the companion uses the map (proposal)

- **Start with the core.** Lead with the Soulfire core principles for the Step, and draw on the others when they fit the person's struggle.
- **One principle at a time.** Name one principle per conversation, not a list.
- **Use their words.** If the person prefers their own words for a principle, use those. "Showing up" for commitment is one example.
- **Pair it with action.** Pair each principle with one small action (see Section 5 of the dossier).
- **Offer a direction, not a verdict.** When someone names a pattern, offer the opposite principle as a direction to move in.
- **No scoring.** Practising a principle is never treated as a score or as proof of progress.

## How It Works: addiction-aware and everyday practice

**Soulfire extension, 5 November 2026 (UTC).** The original map above is preserved. This section adds original application prompts and practices; it is not a replacement for NA literature, a line-by-line rewrite of the chapter, or an NA-endorsed adaptation.

### Source and scope

The primary chapter source is NA World Services' [*An Introductory Guide to Narcotics Anonymous*](https://na.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/05/1200_Introductory-Guide-English.pdf), printed pages 28–101 (PDF pages 36–109). Its publication note explicitly identifies the complete chapter as reprinted from *Narcotics Anonymous*, including the 2008 copyright. The corresponding chapter in the sixth-edition *Basic Text* is Chapter Four, printed pages 17–53. The guide is a chapter source, not the complete Basic Text. Source PDFs and retrieval details are kept in the project's ignored private-reference folder, outside the published app and version history.

The source's broad progression is admission and help, honest examination, readiness for change, repair, and continued practice. The applications below also build on the creator's interpretations and the exercises already in this map. They are suggestions to test with people, not evidence that Soulfire improves clinical outcomes.

**Keep both contexts available.** When a person identifies an addiction or recovery concern, retain their language about addiction, cravings, using, staying clean, recovery, sponsors, meetings, or a Higher Power when it fits. For everyday struggles, use language about habits, relationships, control, avoidance, values, and support. Ask which language helps when unclear. Do not label someone an addict, presume that a frustrating habit is an addiction, or treat all addictions as medically equivalent. NA's chapter addresses drug addiction; applications to other addictions are Soulfire proposals.

Use the same underlying principle with context-sensitive wording. Everyday use should not erase recovery needs, and recovery language should not turn ordinary distress into a diagnosis. Human recovery support and appropriate professional care remain available alongside reflection. Do not advise stopping prescribed medication, attempting unsafe withdrawal, or substituting spiritual exercises for treatment.

### Step 1: notice the pattern before its costs grow

**Source distinction.** Step One describes physical, mental, and spiritual aspects of addiction. It identifies eleven consequences: denial, substitution, rationalization, justification, distrust of others, guilt, embarrassment, dereliction, degradation, isolation, and loss of control. It does not label this list a spiritual-relapse checklist or arrange these aspects into three relapse stages. See the guide's printed page 35 (PDF page 43), corresponding to Basic Text page 20.

**Soulfire application.** A person can use changes in these patterns as a reason to pause and seek support before a familiar difficulty grows. That is an adaptation, not a diagnostic or predictive instrument. No score, required order, threshold, or inevitability is implied. Guilt, embarrassment, fatigue, and time alone can have ordinary or healthy explanations; context and change from the person's own usual pattern matter.

| Source term | An everyday example to explore, not infer | A small response to consider |
|---|---|---|
| Denial | Insisting that everything is fine while repeatedly snapping at people at home. | Describe one recent event and its cost without arguing about what it says about your character. |
| Substitution | Leaving one avoidance habit only to spend the same time compulsively checking another feed or inbox. | Ask what feeling or task is still being avoided, and choose one way to meet that need directly. |
| Rationalization | Explaining repeated missed deadlines as proof that you work best under pressure. | Compare the explanation with what happened the last two times. Make one smaller commitment you can keep. |
| Justification | Treating a difficult day as permission to take frustration out on someone else. | Acknowledge the strain and choose a response that does not pass its cost to another person. |
| Distrust of others | Dismissing every concern from trusted people before considering whether any part is useful. | Consider one specific observation from a safe person. Keep boundaries with anyone whose behavior is genuinely unsafe. |
| Guilt | Letting an actual mistake become a repeating accusation against your whole self. | Identify any concrete harm and possible repair. Separate responsibility for an action from a verdict on your worth. |
| Embarrassment | Hiding a manageable problem because asking for help would mean admitting you are struggling. | Tell one trustworthy person the smallest honest version of what you need. |
| Dereliction | Letting an avoidant cycle repeatedly displace agreed responsibilities or basic care. | Select one essential task, reduce its size, and ask for help. Check capacity, illness, disability, and overload before moralizing. |
| Degradation | Continuing a pattern that conflicts with your dignity or accepting mistreatment as all you deserve. | Name a boundary or support need. Harm done to you is not your character defect or your fault. |
| Isolation | Withdrawing from people who usually help you feel supported because you do not want the pattern noticed. | Make one low-pressure contact. Deliberate solitude and necessary rest are different from unwanted disconnection. |
| Loss of control | Repeatedly breaking your own limit and being unable to interrupt a behavior despite mounting costs. | Bring in human support and reconsider the plan. Substance-related concerns deserve addiction-informed help, not a test of willpower. |

**Three possible check-in lenses, not relapse stages.** If useful to the person, explore values and connection, thoughts and attention, and bodily needs and actions. They can overlap and need not occur in any sequence. These everyday lenses are Soulfire's language, not a clinical translation of NA's disease model.

- **Values and connection:** Where am I moving away from honesty, care, or a practice that helps me? What would reconnect me today? Pain, doubt, or religious disagreement alone do not show spiritual failure.
- **Thoughts and attention:** What story or preoccupation keeps taking over? What am I bargaining with myself about? Can a trusted person help me check the story?
- **Bodily needs and actions:** What have I actually been doing, and what basic support do I need? Notice sleep, food, rest, and repeated behaviors without calling ordinary tiredness a relapse.

**A brief return-to-practice exercise:** Name one observable pattern, one real cost, one choice still available, and one person or resource that could help. Choose a small next action rather than demanding a complete life reset. In addiction recovery, connect this reflection to the person's existing recovery or treatment plan and human supports. If they report an urge to use or a return to use, respond to that directly rather than continuing an abstract values exercise.

### Applications across all twelve steps

The wording and practices in this section are original Soulfire proposals. The page references identify where to consult the source; they do not make each proposed example an NA teaching. Keep the original map's safeguards, particularly around abuse, consent, and amends.

#### Step 1 application: admission with agency

- **Shared core:** Honesty and acceptance.
- **Addiction-aware wording:** Where is addiction pulling you toward something you do not want, and who can help you with that today?
- **Everyday wording:** What keeps happening despite your attempts to force a different result, and what is still yours to choose?
- **Practice:** Use the early-warning reflection above, then the existing Control, Influence, Acceptance, One Action exercise. Recognizing a limit should open access to help, not erase agency or excuse harm.
- **Source location:** Guide printed pages 33–40, PDF pages 41–48.

#### Step 2 application: make room for help

- **Shared core:** Hope and open-mindedness.
- **Addiction-aware wording:** What person, fellowship, professional support, or understanding of a Higher Power helps make recovery feel possible to you?
- **Everyday wording:** Who or what could help you see a way forward that your current thinking is missing?
- **Higher Power invitation:** Would you be open to exploring what a Higher Power of your own understanding might mean for you? The person defines that meaning; Soulfire does not supply a deity, religious figure, or required belief. A particular religious understanding is welcome if it is theirs, but it is not a condition of taking this step.
- **Follow their receptivity:** Offer the invitation gently and follow their words. If they are curious, explore what support, guidance, or connection beyond their own thinking could mean in their life. If they already describe a spiritual path, give the Higher Power dimension more space in that language. If they are unsure or not ready, support that without persuasion or repeated invitations. A secular path is valid, not an incomplete version of the step.
- **Practice:** Identify one concrete example of help that made a difference, then one support to approach. If the Higher Power idea resonates, ask what a small act of openness or trust would look like today. Otherwise, work with a person, community, practice, or value they already find supportive. Belief can remain tentative, and no particular outcome is promised.
- **Bridge to Step 3:** Step 2 begins opening to help from people and, if the person is receptive, a Higher Power of their own understanding. It introduces that possibility without demanding certainty. Step 3 deepens this into turning their will and life over to the care of that Higher Power, paired with the practical actions still theirs to take. Follow the person's readiness and chosen language; this spiritual progression does not make a secular route less valid or require abandoning responsibility.
- **Source location:** Guide printed pages 40–44, PDF pages 48–52. The invitation and pacing above are Soulfire's proposed application, not quoted NA guidance or implemented app behavior.

#### Step 3 application: loosen the demand and choose an action

- **Shared core:** Trust, surrender, and willingness.
- **Addiction-aware wording:** What would following your recovery supports look like in the next difficult moment, rather than trying to manage it alone?
- **Everyday wording:** What outcome are you trying to guarantee, and what useful action can you take without that guarantee?
- **Practice:** Write the desired outcome in one line and the next action in another. Place responsibility with the action, while allowing other people their own choices. Surrender does not require staying in danger or abandoning practical responsibilities.
- **Source location:** Guide printed pages 44–48, PDF pages 52–56.

#### Step 4 application: examine without prosecuting yourself

- **Shared core:** Honesty, courage, and discernment.
- **Addiction-aware wording:** What patterns connected to your addiction or recovery would you like to understand with your sponsor or another appropriate support?
- **Everyday wording:** In one difficult situation, what happened, what did you feel, what did you assume, and what did you do?
- **Practice:** Use those four questions, then add what you handled well and what was outside your control. Work at a tolerable pace. Do not search for a person's contribution to abuse or make a distressed person recount trauma to complete an exercise.
- **Source location:** Guide printed pages 49–56, PDF pages 57–64.

#### Step 5 application: let a safe person know

- **Shared core:** Trust and self-honesty.
- **Addiction-aware wording:** Who could help you speak honestly about the pattern without minimizing it or shaming you?
- **Everyday wording:** Who could hear this with care and help you see it more clearly?
- **Practice:** Choose a suitable person, decide what support you want, and prepare a short opening. Discuss privacy and readiness rather than forcing disclosure. Soulfire can help someone prepare; an AI conversation is not a replacement for the human relationship described in this step.
- **Source location:** Guide printed pages 57–62, PDF pages 65–70.

#### Step 6 application: become willing to try a different response

- **Shared core:** Willingness and self-acceptance.
- **Addiction-aware wording:** Which familiar pattern makes your recovery harder, and what makes it difficult to let go of?
- **Everyday wording:** What does this habit do for you in the moment, and what does it cost afterward?
- **Practice:** Name the short-term payoff, the longer-term cost, and a different response that could serve the underlying need. Choose a modest experiment using the existing opposite-principles table. Do not describe symptoms of illness, disability, or trauma as character defects.
- **Source location:** Guide printed pages 62–65, PDF pages 70–73.

#### Step 7 application: ask specifically and practice patiently

- **Shared core:** Humility and patience.
- **Addiction-aware wording:** What help would support the change you are trying to make in recovery?
- **Everyday wording:** What are you willing to ask for instead of expecting yourself to solve this alone?
- **Practice:** Form one specific request to a person, or a prayer if that fits. Pair it with a doable change in behavior. Review what happened with curiosity, including any feedback from a safe person. A setback can call for a better plan or more support rather than harsher self-judgment.
- **Source location:** Guide printed pages 66–69, PDF pages 74–77.

#### Step 8 application: identify impact before planning contact

- **Shared core:** Honesty, compassion, and willingness.
- **Addiction-aware wording:** Whose trust or wellbeing has your behavior affected, including during active addiction, and what impact can you acknowledge?
- **Everyday wording:** Who has carried a cost from something you did or left undone?
- **Practice:** Record the person, the specific action, and the likely impact. Mark uncertainty rather than inventing responsibility. Becoming willing to address harm is separate from deciding to contact someone. Do not put yourself at fault for setting a healthy boundary or for being harmed.
- **Source location:** Guide printed pages 70–74, PDF pages 78–82.

#### Step 9 application: repair with care for consequences

- **Shared core:** Responsibility and care for the other.
- **Addiction-aware wording:** What repair would support recovery and respect the safety and boundaries of everyone involved?
- **Everyday wording:** What could make the practical impact of your action better for the other person?
- **Practice:** Consider changed conduct, restitution, an appropriate apology, or an indirect repair. Check whether contact is welcome and safe, and seek experienced guidance where needed. Respect no-contact boundaries and legal restrictions. Repair does not require forgiveness, reconciliation, or a favorable reply.
- **Source location:** Guide printed pages 75–79, PDF pages 83–87.

#### Step 10 application: catch a small drift and adjust

- **Shared core:** Honesty and integrity.
- **Addiction-aware wording:** What strengthened your recovery today, and what deserves attention before it becomes harder to manage?
- **Everyday wording:** Where did your actions fit your values today, and where would one adjustment help?
- **Practice:** Choose one thing to keep, one thing to change, and any actual harm to repair. Revisit Step 1 patterns when useful without turning the list into continuous self-surveillance. Avoid repeated apologies for the same settled matter or treating every uncomfortable feeling as wrongdoing.
- **Source location:** Guide printed pages 79–83, PDF pages 87–91.

#### Step 11 application: listen, discern, and return to life

- **Shared core:** Commitment, humility, and spiritual connection.
- **Addiction-aware wording:** What prayer, meditation, or reflective practice helps you stay connected to your recovery and the support you rely on?
- **Everyday wording:** What helps you pause long enough to notice the kind of person you want to be in this situation?
- **Practice:** Try a brief prayer, quiet reflection, or grounded pause chosen by the person. Ask what guidance and strength would help, then name an action that expresses it. Wandering thoughts are not failure, and an uncomfortable practice can be changed or stopped.
- **Source comparison:** The earlier theology note remains above as historical context. The Basic Text adds nuance: its Step Eleven discussion describes a developing alignment between God's will and a person's deeper values, while distinguishing those values from immediate wants. This supports exploring the creator's view without claiming that every preferred outcome is divine guidance.
- **Source location:** Guide printed pages 83–93, PDF pages 91–101; the alignment passage is printed page 91, PDF page 99.

#### Step 12 application: offer what helps without taking over

- **Shared core:** Service and care.
- **Addiction-aware wording:** What part of your recovery experience could you offer to someone who wants support, while continuing to care for your own recovery?
- **Everyday wording:** What small useful thing can you offer without needing to control how it is received?
- **Practice:** Choose a bounded act of help, check that it is welcome, and stay within your capacity. Let changed behavior carry the lesson. Do not recruit, promise to rescue someone, or use helping others to avoid your own needs and responsibilities.
- **Source location:** Guide printed pages 94–101, PDF pages 102–109.

### Using this extension in a conversation

Start with the person's situation and preferred language, not a step number or the whole checklist. Offer one reflection or practice, then check whether it fits. When addiction is central, keep it central and support connection with appropriate people and care. When the issue is an everyday conflict or habit, use the broader wording without assigning an addiction identity. These are content proposals only; no app behavior, diagnosis, risk score, or automatic relapse detection is specified here.

## From a person's situation to a chosen practice

**Reviewable product design, not an implementation.** Combine the step map, the creator's broader wisdom, and the person's actual needs into one coherent response. The goal is understanding followed by useful guidance and a relevant choice, rather than classifying someone and assigning a worksheet.

### Proposed conversation flow

1. **Understand the situation and the request.** Reflect the concrete difficulty in ordinary language. Separate stated facts, feelings, interpretations, and any real responsibility or deadline. Ask one necessary question when something material is missing. Answer a direct practical question before offering an exercise. A complete answer can be enough.
2. **Notice possible step connections provisionally.** One situation can relate to several steps. Resentment may invite Step 4 reflection, Step 3 release of an outcome, and Step 10 repair, with Step 11 quiet if wanted. Offer a likely starting point with a short reason, then let the person correct it. They can use multiple principles without following a fixed sequence or completing earlier steps first.
3. **Choose language and depth with the person.** Retain addiction and recovery terms when those describe the concern. Use broader everyday wording when that fits. Independently respect spiritual, secular, or uncertain language preferences. Someone can want addiction-specific support without theological language, or spiritual reflection about an ordinary life problem. Do not infer either preference from the other.
4. **Offer grounded guidance.** Use the relevant step's principle and, where appropriate, a reviewed wisdom lens to illuminate the situation. Keep uncertainty visible. A source can offer a question; it cannot establish another person's motives, tell the user what their hidden trauma is, or decide what a Higher Power intends.
5. **Offer a useful choice, if a practice would help.** Name what it is for, whether it exists in the app, and a rough scope. Options include conversation only, an available interactive practice, quiet, Ember, optional Current music, or an off-app action or person. Offer a small number of relevant choices, not the whole feature catalogue. Declining must not trigger another sales pitch.
6. **Let the person choose and remain in control.** Selection, starting audio, sharing a reflection, saving, and contacting anyone are distinct actions. Do not start music or a timer because a step was inferred. Do not silently replace their active practice or route private answers into a new model request.
7. **Connect the result to life.** Ask what, if anything, they want to carry forward. Support a concrete action, a boundary, a conversation with a human, rest, or simply ending. Revisit only when there is new information or the person wants a different kind of help. Relief, agreement with the companion, and exercise completion are not proof of recovery.

### How broader wisdom belongs in this flow

Use the Twelve Steps as the organizing foundation and the [existing additional-perspectives draft](additional-perspectives.md) as a set of optional interpretive lenses. The draft already describes compassionate curiosity, emotional honesty, pausing before reaction, awareness of patterns, and reflection on motives. It distinguishes Jung, Maté, Ghiyam, and Hudson rather than pretending their frameworks are interchangeable. Its underlying quotations and source claims are not yet independently verified.

For material inspired by the creator's Wisdom Vault, record which approved source or note supports a proposed insight before attributing it. Keep three things separate: the source's teaching, the creator's interpretation, and the Soulfire practice we propose. If a relevant vault source has not been reviewed, label that connection as a candidate for review and rely on the established map in the meantime. Do not fabricate a vault quotation or claim a teacher endorses a practice.

The design should work from a reviewed, versioned set of project guidance. It does not require connecting to the private vault at runtime, copying private reflections into the repository, or making an external retrieval call for each response. The eventual content set and any runtime integration would be separate work. The present addition changes no prompt, schema, interface, or vault connection.

### Three examples for review

**Resentment after an ordinary disagreement.** A person feels dismissed and keeps rehearsing an argument. First understand what was said, what they need now, and whether the relationship is safe. A possible connection is Step 4 for understanding the resentment, Step 3 for an outcome they cannot dictate, and Step 10 if they identify conduct of their own to repair. Ask about their feelings, assumptions, choices, and needs without presuming fault. They may conclude that a boundary matters or that nothing further is theirs to own. Offer the current Step 10 reflection only when that fits; the dedicated Step 4 worksheet remains proposed. Ember or quiet can be offered if they want space before deciding, never as a requirement to tolerate harm. Where there is abuse or coercion, prioritize safety and specialist or trusted human support rather than an ordinary own-part exercise.

**Noticing a familiar recovery or avoidance pattern.** A person reports hiding something, avoiding support, and bargaining with themselves. Explore the actual behavior and what they are asking for; do not announce that they are in a relapse stage. Steps 1, 2, 3, and 10 may offer useful perspectives. If addiction is involved, retain recovery language and connect the reflection to existing human support and protective plans. An available urge/support practice may fit after they choose it, but meditation or music should not replace immediate help for a substance-related risk. In a non-addiction context, use the same pause to identify the avoided need or responsibility and one manageable action.

**Wanting guidance rather than another explanation.** A person asks for a way to quiet down and reconnect with what matters. Step 11 may fit, possibly with Step 3 if they are gripping a particular outcome. Offer a brief prayer in their language, a secular intention, the quiet timer, or Ember. Current is an optional sound choice, not an assumed background layer. Let them select a practice and stop or change it. Afterward, one chosen action or ending the session is enough; no compulsory insight, affirmation, or progress score is needed.

### Review checks before any implementation

- The same real-life situation can reasonably connect to more than one step, and the person can reject the interpretation.
- Addiction-aware versus everyday language and spiritual versus secular language remain independent choices.
- Direct practical help is not withheld until the person completes reflection, quiet, or a lesson.
- The Step 1 list is attributed accurately and never presented as a validated warning-sign score, a diagnosis, or a sequence of relapse stages.
- Step 4 reflection permits no fault to own and never assigns responsibility for abuse to the person harmed.
- Every offered action is identified as implemented, currently unavailable, off-app, or proposed. Step 4 and multi-step exercise generation are not misrepresented as live features.
- Ember, Current, timers, sharing, saving, and contacting others require the person's relevant choice. This document starts none of them.
- Source claims can be traced to a reviewed reference, while private vault material and source PDFs stay out of public code and runtime prompts unless separately authorized and appropriate.
- A useful session may finish with a practical action, support from another person, or a decision to do nothing further. No new monitoring, reminder, or app implementation is implied.

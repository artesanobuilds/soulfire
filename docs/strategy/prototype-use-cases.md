# Four prototype journeys

The creator selected addiction, anxiety, depression, and relationship conflict as the first areas to explore. These scenarios make that direction concrete. They are fictional design examples, not diagnoses, treatment protocols, or claims of effectiveness.

The product should respond to the person's experience rather than requiring a category or diagnostic label. Spiritual framing is offered in the person's language. Suggested principles and exercises remain revisable and optional.

Across all four journeys, the person can type, dictate an editable draft, or use live voice. Test opening by voice, switching to an exercise field, and returning to conversation without losing context. Live voice transmission and draft submission have distinct consent and privacy implications; spoken input must not automatically share private exercise fields. Spoken responses can be muted while text remains available.

## 1. Addiction: returning to a pattern despite its costs

**Opening:** "Every night I tell myself I won't gamble, then I open the betting app. I lost money again and haven't told my partner."

**Situation:** evening stress, a recurring urge, costly behavior, and shame. The first useful goal is one supported response to the next urge, not a declaration that the person is cured or a demand for a full confession.

**Principles and steps:** honesty and acceptance (1), willingness and trust (3), awareness and asking for help (7). Human connection is part of the next action.

**Generated exercise: Before the next urge**

- A short reflection: when does the urge tend to arrive, and what does the behavior seem to offer?
- Three cards: "What arrives," "What it costs," and "What I can do next." Entries are editable; no diagnosis is inferred.
- Choose a practical barrier, such as activating a gambling block or self-exclusion tool, and a trusted person or relevant support service to contact.
- Optionally add a brief pause or prayer for strength.
- Write an if-then plan: "If I reach for the betting app tonight, I put the phone down, use the barrier I chose, and contact someone who can support me."

**Personalization:** time of day, triggers, preferred language, available support, and an action the person is actually willing to take. Do not suggest gambling in moderation or treat a short meditation as sufficient protection.

**Boundaries:** do not push immediate disclosure to a partner without considering context. If the conversation involves substance dependence or withdrawal, do not generate a do-it-yourself detox plan. Acute danger or self-harm needs appropriate human help.

**Useful outcome to explore:** whether the person felt less shamed and took a concrete protective or connection step. Do not interpret one completed exercise as recovery.

## 2. Anxiety: rehearsing a feared future

**Opening:** "I have a presentation tomorrow. I keep imagining myself freezing and everyone realizing I'm not good enough."

**Situation:** anticipatory worry, uncertainty, repeated mental rehearsal, and a practical event to prepare for.

**Principles and steps:** acceptance (1), surrender and willingness (3), faith or trust in the person's own language (11).

**Generated exercise: Prepare what is yours; release the verdict**

- Sort editable cards into "I can prepare," "I can influence," and "I can't decide for others."
- Name the feeling without promising to eliminate it.
- Choose one preparation task: rehearse the opening, simplify a slide, or arrange notes.
- Optional short eyes-open meditation or prayer for steadiness; stopping is always available.
- Choose an if-then action: "If I lose my place, I pause and look at my next note."

**Personalization:** the actual feared event, preparation already completed, time available, and desired spiritual framing. Avoid endless reassurance that the feared outcome cannot happen.

**Boundaries:** not every anxiety is a distorted fear. Real threats call for practical support. New or severe physical symptoms must not be assumed to be anxiety. Destabilizing inward attention calls for stopping or adapting the practice.

**Useful outcome to explore:** whether the person can identify a manageable preparation step and proceed with room for uncertainty.

## 3. Depression: low mood and loss of momentum

**Opening:** "I've felt flat for weeks. I barely leave the house, and even small things feel pointless."

**Situation:** low energy, isolation, reduced meaning, and possible depression. Respond gently and understand severity; do not frame the struggle as insufficient faith, a character defect, or a discipline failure.

**Principles and steps:** hope and connection (2), willingness and self-acceptance (6), compassion expressed through a very small action. Avoid a defect or harm inventory as the opening practice.

**Generated exercise: One small act of care**

- Offer a very short choice: "Would support, rest, or one small action feel most helpful right now?"
- Select a feasible action, such as drinking water, opening the curtains, or messaging a trusted person.
- If welcome, reflect on a source of connection or meaning without forcing gratitude or positivity.
- Create an optional plan anchored to an existing moment: "After I finish this cup of tea, I'll open the curtains."
- End without a performance score; allow stopping, postponing, or asking for help.

**Personalization:** energy, available support, practical constraints, and whether spiritual language helps or hurts today. Meditation is optional, not the default answer.

**Boundaries:** persistent or impairing low mood deserves encouragement toward qualified care. Ask about immediate safety when context warrants it; suicidal intent or immediate danger interrupts the exercise for location-appropriate urgent support. Do not require a diagnostic label to take distress seriously.

**Useful outcome to explore:** whether the person felt met with care and found an achievable action or connection. This is not a measure of depression treatment.

## 4. Relationship conflict: anger, hurt, and repair

**Opening:** "My partner says I'm always on my phone. I snapped and said something cruel. I'm still angry, but I don't want us to keep doing this."

**Situation:** a recurring disagreement, hurt feelings, and a specific action the person regrets. Explore safety and context without assuming mutual blame or that reconciliation is the right outcome.

**Principles and steps:** honesty, courage, and discernment (4), compassion and care for the other (8–9), integrity and appropriate repair (10).

**Generated exercise: What happened; what is mine; what comes next**

- Separate the event, feeling, and interpretation into editable reflection cards.
- Offer ownership of the person's own words or actions without requiring responsibility for the other person's conduct.
- Let the person identify a need or boundary, and mark that nothing further is theirs to own.
- If appropriate, draft a brief repair: "I'm sorry I said that. It was hurtful. I'd like to talk about the phone issue when we're both ready."
- Consider timing, consent, and possible harm. Copying a draft is optional; the app never sends it.

**Personalization:** what was said, what matters to the person, boundaries, and readiness to talk. Avoid inventing the partner's motives or demanding forgiveness.

**Boundaries:** coercion, abuse, threats, and no-contact requests change the path to safety, boundaries, and human support. Do not apply an ordinary-conflict "my part" exercise or recommend contact in those circumstances.

**Useful outcome to explore:** whether the person gains clarity about an appropriate repair or boundary, rather than whether the relationship is preserved.

## Design and evaluation implications

The same system should produce genuinely different experiences: protective planning for an addictive pattern, sorting and preparation for anxiety, a gentle low-effort choice for low mood, and discernment plus repair for conflict. Do not funnel all four into the same meditation worksheet.

Build these on a shared exercise schema and component library. Keep all twelve steps as the program's map; let the first useful practice draw on relevant steps without requiring a person to complete the whole sequence first.

Evaluate variant cases: spiritual belief and non-belief, user corrections, refusal to participate, no available buddy, severe distress, harmful situations, and malformed generated output. Test whether personalization is accurate, actions are manageable, private drafts remain private, and the person can easily pause or decline.

Before an external pilot, obtain appropriate review of sensitive flows. Feedback on usefulness and usability does not establish clinical efficacy.

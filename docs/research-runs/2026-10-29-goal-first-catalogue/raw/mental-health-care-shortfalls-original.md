# Where Mental Health Care Falls Short: The 20 Biggest Struggles Worldwide, and Why Therapy Often Isn't Enough

The single most important finding is that therapy's biggest failure is not that it doesn't work. It is that most people never get an effective dose of it. Globally, only 6.9% of people with a mental or substance use disorder receive effective treatment.\[1\] Even in good conditions, standard psychotherapies produce a 50% symptom reduction in only about a third to two-fifths of patients:\[2\] 42% for depression, 32–38% for anxiety disorders, PTSD and OCD, and 24% for borderline personality disorder.

## TL;DR
- **The scale:** More than 1 billion people (about 1 in 7) live with a mental disorder. Anxiety and depression are the most common in both sexes, and suicide claimed about 727,000 lives in 2021 (WHO, 2025). The 20 biggest struggles cluster into three groups. Group A is conditions where therapy has a low ceiling: chronic or treatment-resistant depression, PTSD (especially in veterans), eating disorders, borderline personality disorder, OCD and co-occurring addiction. Group B is populations therapy reaches poorly: teens and young adults, LGBTQ+ youth, new mothers, people in poverty, and people in low- and middle-income countries. Group C is problems therapy isn't designed to fix: loneliness, chronic stress and burnout, and deprivation.
- **How therapy fails once people are in it:** Response rates are modest. Gains fade, with the depression treatment advantage dropping from a relative risk of 1.68 at 3 months to 1.14 at 5 years. Real-world attrition is massive: in England's NHS Talking Therapies, 1.81 million referrals led to just 670,419 completed courses (about 37%).\[3\]\[4\] Outcomes are worst for the most deprived (40.5% recovery vs 52.4% for the least deprived), for under-18s (41.2%) and for PTSD (41.9%).\[5\]\[6\]
- **How therapy fails to reach people:** Only 1 in 27 people with depression in low- and lower-middle-income countries gets minimally adequate treatment, against 1 in 5 in high-income countries.\[7\] In the US, only 19.3% of people who need substance use treatment receive it.\[8\] Half of LGBTQ+ young people who wanted care couldn't get it. The fix is not "more of the same therapy." It is measurement-based stepped care, integrated addiction and mental health treatment, task-shared community care, and interventions that target social determinants such as isolation, poverty and stress.

## Key Findings

**1. Global burden.** WHO's *World mental health today* (September 2025) estimates that more than 1 billion people live with mental disorders, which are the second-leading cause of long-term disability.\[9\] Depression and anxiety alone cost the global economy about US$1 trillion a year.\[9\] Women are disproportionately affected overall.\[10\] WHO's Mental Health Atlas 2024 puts the global median workforce at 13.5 specialised mental health workers per 100,000 people, with fewer than three in low-income countries and more than 67 in high-income countries; in low-income countries fewer than 10% of affected individuals receive care, compared with over 50% in higher-income nations.

**2. The treatment cascade collapses at every step.** In a *JAMA Psychiatry* analysis of 56,927 World Mental Health survey respondents in 21 countries (Vigo et al.), attrition was severe at each stage:\[11\]\[12\]
- Only 46.5% of people with a disorder recognized they needed treatment.
- Of those, only 34.1% sought help from services.
- Of those who sought help, 82.9% got minimally adequate care.
- Of those, only about 47% received effective treatment.\[12\]\[13\]\[14\]

The net result is 6.9% effectively treated. For alcohol use disorder, the figure was just 1.4%.\[11\]

**3. Even ideal therapy leaves most people symptomatic.** The largest cross-disorder meta-analysis (Cuijpers et al., *World Psychiatry* 2024; 441 trials, 33,881 patients) found these response rates for psychotherapy:\[15\]

| Disorder | Psychotherapy response rate |
|---|---|
| Major depression | 0.42 |
| PTSD | 0.38 |
| OCD | 0.38 |
| Panic disorder | 0.38 |
| GAD | 0.36 |
| Social anxiety | 0.32 |
| Specific phobia | 0.32 |
| Borderline personality disorder | 0.24 |

Response in control conditions ranged from 0.05 (OCD) to 0.19 (depression).\[16\] Therapy clearly beats doing nothing, but in trials, a majority of patients do not reach a 50% symptom reduction.

**4. Effects fade over time.** A 2026 *World Psychiatry* meta-analysis of long-term depression outcomes found that therapy's advantage over control peaks at 12 weeks (standardized mean difference, SMD, 0.55) and declines to 0.39 at about one year.\[17\] The relative risk of response falls from 1.68 at 3 months to 1.14 at 5 years.\[17\] Benefits persist, but they shrink.

## The Top 20: Ranked by Burden × Therapy Shortfall

The ranking weighs three things: how many people are affected, how severe the harm is, and how large the gap is between what therapy delivers and what people need. Items 1–10 are clinical problem patterns. Items 11–20 are populations and contexts where therapy systematically under-delivers.

### 1. Depression that doesn't remit: chronic, recurrent and treatment-resistant
- **Burden:** Depression and anxiety are the two most common disorder types worldwide.\[10\]
- **How care falls short:**
  - The best-studied therapies get a response in only 42% of trial patients.
  - In NIMH's landmark STAR*D trial, remission rates fell with each treatment step: about 37%, 31%, 14% and 13%.\[18\] About a third of patients failed to remit after two adequate trials, which defines treatment-resistant depression.\[19\]
  - Patients who needed more steps had higher relapse rates.\[20\]
  - The long-term advantage of therapy shrinks, from a relative risk of 1.68 at 3 months to 1.14 at 5 years.
- **Nuance:** Short courses (6–8 sessions) are built for single episodes, but more than 75% of STAR*D patients had recurrent or chronic depression.\[21\] Adjunctive psychotherapy in treatment-resistant depression roughly doubled remission (RR 1.9), but few systems routinely combine the two.\[22\]

### 2. Anxiety disorders: GAD, panic and social anxiety
- **Burden:** Anxiety disorders are the most prevalent mental disorders worldwide.\[23\]
- **How care falls short:**
  - Psychotherapy response is 0.36 for GAD, 0.38 for panic and only 0.32 for social anxiety.
  - Social anxiety is a cruel mismatch. The disorder makes it hard to phone a clinic, attend groups or talk to a stranger, which is exactly what therapy requires.
  - In NHS Talking Therapies prediction modelling, reliable recovery for anxiety was 49.2%, so about half of completers don't reliably recover.\[24\]

### 3. Adolescent depression, anxiety and hopelessness
- **Burden (US, CDC YRBS):**
  - 40% of high-school students reported persistent sadness or hopelessness in 2023, up from 30% in 2013.\[25\]
  - Rates were 53% among girls and about 65% among LGBTQ+ students.\[26\]
  - The 2025 YRBS shows improvement to 33% overall, though 43% of girls are still affected.\[27\] This is an encouraging but recent trend.
- **How care falls short:**
  - Youth therapy for depression has the weakest evidence base. Weisz's 2006 meta-analysis found a mean effect size of only 0.34, and a 13-year update found 0.29 at post-treatment and 0.22 at follow-up, with no improvement over time.\[28\]\[29\]
  - In Weisz's 50-year meta-analysis, effects for depression and conduct problems actually *declined* across decades.\[30\]
  - In NHS Talking Therapies 2025/26, under-18s recovered at 41.2% and 18–25-year-olds at 44.1%, versus 50.4% for ages 26–64.\[6\]
- **Nuance:** Adult CBT protocols scaled down for teens often ignore the school, family and online environments where symptoms are generated.

### 4. Suicidal thoughts and behaviour
- **Burden:**
  - Suicide caused about 727,000 deaths globally in 2021 and is a leading cause of death among young people (WHO).\[31\]
  - In the US, 20% of high-schoolers seriously considered suicide and 9% attempted it in 2023. Among LGBTQ+ students, the figures were 41% and 20%.\[32\]\[33\]
- **How care falls short:**
  - Most talk therapy treats suicidality as a symptom of depression rather than targeting it directly.
  - The highest-risk moments, at night and in crisis, fall between weekly sessions.
  - LGBTQ+ young people report fear of discussing concerns as a top barrier to care.

### 5. Addiction and substance use disorders, especially alcohol
- **Burden (US, NSDUH 2024):** 16.8% of people aged 12 or older (48.4 million) had a substance use disorder.\[34\]
- **How care falls short:**
  - Only 19.3% of those needing treatment received any.
  - Just 2.5% of the 27.9 million people with alcohol use disorder received medication for it.\[8\]
  - Globally, only 11.8% of people with lifetime alcohol use disorder ever sought treatment, and only 1.4% got effective treatment.\[35\]\[36\]
- **Nuance:** The biggest barrier is not therapy quality but perceived need. In 2022–23 NSDUH data, only 5.5% of untreated people with a substance use disorder felt they needed treatment.\[37\] Weekly talk therapy is rarely the right front door for someone who doesn't see a problem.

### 6. Co-occurring mental illness and addiction
- **Burden (US):** 21.2 million adults had both a mental illness and a substance use disorder in 2024.
- **How care falls short:**
  - 41.2% of these adults received *no* treatment of either kind.
  - Only 14.5% received treatment for both conditions.
  - Among adolescents with depression plus a substance use disorder, 27.9% got nothing and only 18.1% got both.\[38\]
- **Nuance:** This is a structural failure. Mental health and addiction services are funded and run separately. Patients are told to "get sober first" or "stabilize your mood first," and fall between the two.

### 7. PTSD and complex trauma, especially in veterans
- **How care falls short:**
  - In US military and veteran trials, about two-thirds (60–72%) of patients receiving first-line cognitive processing therapy or prolonged exposure still met PTSD criteria after treatment (Steenkamp et al., *JAMA* 2015).\[39\]
  - Trauma-focused therapies were only marginally superior to non-trauma-focused comparison therapies.\[40\]
  - In one trial of female veterans and active-duty personnel, dropout from prolonged exposure was 38%, versus 21% for present-centred therapy.\[39\]
  - In a 709-veteran cohort, PTSD remission was just 39.4%.\[41\]
  - In NHS Talking Therapies (civilians), PTSD recovery was 41.9% in 2025/26.
- **Nuance:** Exposure-based therapy asks people to approach the very memories they organize their lives around avoiding, so dropout is high. Sleep problems often persist even after "successful" treatment.\[42\] A veterans trial found dropout was 43% for in-office therapy versus 25% for in-home delivery, which suggests that logistics drive part of the attrition.\[43\]

### 8. Eating disorders, especially anorexia nervosa
- **How care falls short:**
  - CBT remission rates are about 33% for anorexia, 28% for bulimia and 50% for binge-eating disorder.\[44\]
  - End-of-treatment remission for *adult* anorexia ranges from 0% to 25%. For adolescents it is 23–33%, and only about a third of those remain in remission at four years.\[45\]
  - Average relapse in anorexia is 37% over about 31 months. Higher depression and lower post-treatment BMI predict relapse.\[46\]
  - Anorexia's crude mortality is 5–6%, among the highest in psychiatry.\[45\]
- **Nuance:** The illness is ego-syntonic (people often value the behaviours therapy targets), so motivation is ambivalent. Weight restoration is not the same as psychological recovery.

### 9. Obsessive-compulsive disorder
- **How care falls short:**
  - Response to psychotherapy is 0.38.
  - An estimated 35% of people experience no discernible change after exposure and response prevention (ERP), the gold-standard treatment, and about 50% do not reach remission.\[47\]
  - Dropout from ERP once it starts is a modest 14.7%, but one review found pre-treatment attrition of 30.4%.\[48\]\[49\]\[50\]\[51\]
- **Nuance:** The fear of exposure loses people before therapy begins. Clinicians hold back too: Deacon et al. (*Journal of Anxiety Disorders*, 2013) validated the Therapist Beliefs about Exposure Scale in more than 600 practising therapists and found that "the average clinician harbors a moderate degree of negative beliefs about exposure therapy." A 2023 systematic review in *Cognitive Behaviour Therapy* found these beliefs were linked to less use of exposure in all 14 studies that used the scale.

### 10. Borderline personality disorder and severe emotion dysregulation
- **How care falls short:**
  - BPD has the lowest psychotherapy response of the eight disorders analysed (0.24).
  - It was the only disorder where the relative risk versus control was not significant.\[15\]
- **Nuance:** The evidence-based options (DBT, mentalization-based treatment) are long and intensive, with year-long programmes. Short-course systems offer a fraction of the required dose, and people with BPD are often excluded from primary-care therapy services as "too complex."

### 11. Perinatal and postpartum depression
- **Burden:** Estimates vary sharply by method. An umbrella review found a mean prevalence of 26.3%, while a meta-regression using adjusted estimates found 11.9%.\[52\]\[53\] In low- and middle-income countries, prevalence is about 30% antenatally and 20% postnatally.\[54\]
- **How care falls short:**
  - Only 1 in 10 diagnosed women in low- and middle-income countries receive treatment, and over 90% remain untreated.\[54\]\[55\]
  - In a large US urban medical centre, only 17.1% of women received mental health services. Those who did improved faster and sustained their gains, which shows that the treatment works when delivered.\[56\]
- **Nuance:** Mothers cite fear of custody loss and worries about medication in breastfeeding.\[56\] Weekly appointments are also impractical with a newborn.

### 12. Loneliness and social disconnection
- **Burden (WHO Commission on Social Connection, 2025):**
  - 1 in 6 people worldwide are affected, and loneliness is linked to about 871,000 deaths a year.\[57\]
  - Rates are about 24% in low-income countries versus 11% in high-income countries, and 17–21% among people aged 13–29.\[58\]
  - Gallup finds 22% of employees felt lonely "a lot of the previous day," rising to 27% among fully remote workers.\[59\]\[60\]
- **How care falls short:** One hour a week with a paid professional can be a substitute for connection rather than a route to it. Therapy can address the thinking patterns behind isolation, but it cannot create community.

### 13. Chronic stress and burnout
- **Burden:** 40% of employees globally experienced significant stress the previous day, and 50% in the US and Canada, the highest of any region (Gallup 2025–2026).\[61\]
- **How care falls short:** When the cause is workload, insecurity or poor management, individual therapy can teach coping but leaves the stressor untouched. Many people describe this as being "taught to tolerate an intolerable job."

### 14. Deprivation and poverty
- **How care falls short:**
  - In NHS Talking Therapies 2024/25, 52.4% of completers from the least-deprived areas recovered, versus 40.5% from the most-deprived.
  - This gap persists even though more people from deprived areas complete treatment.\[5\]
- **Nuance:** CBT asks people to reappraise threats. When the threats are real (debt, housing, unsafe neighbourhoods), standard protocols fit poorly.

### 15. LGBTQ+ young people
- **Burden:** 39% seriously considered suicide in the past year, including 46% of transgender and nonbinary youth (Trevor Project 2024, a large but non-representative US sample).\[62\]
- **How care falls short:**
  - 84% wanted mental health care, but 50% of those couldn't get it.\[63\]
  - The top reasons were fear of discussing concerns, cost, and not wanting to seek parental permission.\[64\]
  - Some reported being threatened with or subjected to conversion efforts.\[65\]
- **Nuance:** For LGBTQ+ youth, finding an affirming therapist is part of what makes care effective, not an optional extra.

### 16. People in low- and middle-income countries
- **How care falls short:**
  - Only 3.7% of people with depression in low- and lower-middle-income countries received minimally adequate treatment, versus 22.4% in high-income countries.\[66\]
  - Only 1 in 3 people with depression in these countries recognized a need for treatment.\[67\]
- **Nuance:** Therapy works here when delivered. Psychotherapy effect sizes in non-Western trials were large (g = 0.73 after bias adjustment).\[68\] The barrier is workforce and funding, not efficacy. Task-shared models such as the Thinking Healthy Programme, delivered by community health workers, show the way forward.\[69\]

### 17. Dropout and disengagement: the "silent failure"
- **How care falls short:**
  - Meta-analytic dropout from adult psychotherapy is 19.7% in research settings (Swift and Greenberg; 669 studies). An earlier meta-analysis of 125 studies (Wierzbicki and Pekarik, 1993) found a mean dropout rate of 46.86%.
  - In the real world, only about 37% of NHS Talking Therapies referrals finish a course of at least two sessions, a proportion that has barely moved in a decade.
  - Some sites see just 40% of referrals attend two or more treatment sessions.\[70\]
- **Nuance:** People who drop out are poorer and more often from minority groups.\[71\] Most dropouts are never followed up, so the service never learns why it failed.

### 18. Hidden waits and between-session gaps
- **How care falls short:**
  - England reports 91.6% starting treatment within six weeks.\[72\]
  - But only 82.7% of people who reached a second session did so within 18 weeks, against a 95% target.\[72\]
  - These hidden waits are measured only for people who come back, so anyone who gave up isn't counted.\[73\]
- **Nuance:** The time between a first contact and real treatment is often when hope runs out.

### 19. Unrecognized need, low mental-health literacy and stigma
- **How care falls short:**
  - Fewer than half (46.5%) of people with a diagnosable disorder recognize they need help.\[12\]
  - Of those who do, only about a third seek it.\[11\]
  - People with severe depression are more likely to get *some* service but *less* likely to get adequate care.\[74\]
- **Nuance:** Therapy supply cannot fix a demand problem. Outreach, primary-care detection and family education are prerequisites.

### 20. Older adults: under-reached rather than under-responsive
- **Nuance:** This is a counter-intuitive finding. When older adults do reach NHS Talking Therapies, they recover at the highest rates: 61.6% at ages 65–74 and 64.7% at 75–89, versus 50.4% at ages 26–64.\[6\]
- **Where the gap lies:** In getting older adults referred, and in addressing the isolation, bereavement and physical illness that drive their distress. US data from 2014 showed only about 21% of adults over 65 needing substance use treatment received it.\[35\]

## Cross-Cutting: Why Therapy Falls Short

1. **The dose is too small for the problem.** Primary-care services average about 8 sessions (8.4 in NHS Talking Therapies), but chronic depression, BPD, eating disorders and complex trauma need far more.\[3\]
2. **Measurement exists but rarely triggers change.** Talking Therapies tracks outcomes session by session, yet half of completers don't recover. STAR*D showed the value of switching or augmenting early, but most services repeat the same approach rather than escalating.
3. **Siloed care.** Mental health and addiction services are separate, so only 14.5% of US adults with both conditions receive integrated help.
4. **Format mismatches.** Weekly, in-office, 50-minute sessions are a poor fit for new mothers, shift workers, people with social anxiety and veterans; home-based delivery cut dropout from 43% to 25%.
5. **Social determinants sit outside the therapy room.** Deprivation cuts recovery by about 12 percentage points. Loneliness and job stress are drivers that therapy cannot remove.
6. **Effects fade without maintenance.** Depression gains shrink over time, and relapse in anorexia is common.
7. **Access gaps dwarf efficacy gaps.** Moving from 6.9% to even 20% effective coverage would help far more people than any new therapy.

## Recommendations

- **For health systems:**
  - Fund stepped, measurement-based care that automatically escalates non-responders by session 4–6 rather than discharging them.
  - Integrate addiction and mental health treatment under one roof, and make medication for alcohol use disorder routine.
- **For services:**
  - Track and contact people who drop out.
  - Offer home, telehealth and flexible formats.
  - Fund longer pathways for BPD, eating disorders and complex trauma.
- **For LMICs and underserved groups:** Scale task-shared, community-delivered interventions with proven large effects.
- **For individuals:** If therapy isn't helping after 6–8 sessions, ask for a different approach (modality, therapist, medication combination) rather than assuming you are "treatment-resistant." Non-response to one approach is common, but it is not a reliable predictor of response to the next.
- **For employers and policymakers:** Address the causes of stress and isolation (workload, social infrastructure) rather than relying on employee assistance counselling alone.

## Caveats

- Global data are thin. The richest outcome data come from the US (NSDUH, YRBS) and England (NHS Talking Therapies), and should not be assumed to generalize.
- The WMH effective-treatment estimates rely on DSM-IV-era surveys.\[12\]
- Trial response rates come from selected samples, and real-world outcomes are generally worse.
- The Trevor Project sample is not nationally representative.\[75\]
- Perinatal prevalence estimates conflict (11.9% vs 26.3%) depending on whether screening scales or diagnostic interviews were used.
- STAR*D's original remission figures are contested. Pigott et al. (*BMJ Open*, 2023) re-analysed the patient-level data using the trial's own protocol and found that 1,089 of 3,110 patients remitted (35.0%), against the 67% originally reported; Rush et al. disputed the reanalysis in the *American Journal of Psychiatry*.
- Some important areas are not ranked here because this research did not gather robust failure-rate data on them: adult ADHD, insomnia, prolonged grief, psychosis/schizophrenia, chronic pain-related distress, and social media effects on teens. They should not be read as less important.
